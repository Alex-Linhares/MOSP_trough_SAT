\section{The pathwidth of sparse random graphs}
\label{app:random}

The equivalences of Section~\ref{sec:equiv} make every statement about the
pathwidth of a graph a statement about the open stacks of an instance. This
appendix proves one such statement for random graphs, in plain language, and
records what of it is checked in Lean. The result is that a sparse random
graph has pathwidth proportional to its number of vertices, with an explicit
constant. The argument is a first moment, an old tool; what is new here is the
exact form of its deterministic step, the constant it yields, and the
machine check. We found no prior statement of the constant.

\subsection{The statement}

$G(n,p)$ is the random graph on $n$ labelled vertices in which each of the
$\binom n2$ possible edges is present with probability $p$, independently. We
take $p=c/n$, so that a vertex has about $c$ neighbours. Write
$\mathrm{pw}$ for pathwidth, $\ln$ for the natural logarithm, and
\[
  \phi(x) = -x\ln x \quad(\phi(0)=0),\qquad
  H(x,y,z) = \phi(x)+\phi(y)+\phi(z)
\]
for the entropy of a probability vector with three entries.

\begin{theorem}[first moment, finite form]
\label{thm:firstmoment}
Let $c>0$ and $0<\beta<1$, and put $a=(1-\beta)/2$ and
\[
  \delta(\beta,c) \;=\; c\,a^{2} \;-\; H(a,a,1-2a).
\]
For every integer $n\ge\max(3,c)$,
\[
  \Pr\bigl[\mathrm{pw}(G(n,c/n))\le\beta n\bigr]
  \;\le\; \exp\bigl(-\delta(\beta,c)\,n+3\ln n+c\,a\bigr)
  \;\le\; e^{c/2}\,n^{3}\,e^{-\delta(\beta,c)\,n}.
\]
\end{theorem}

So whenever $\delta(\beta,c)>0$, the probability that the pathwidth is as small
as $\beta n$ is exponentially small, and the pathwidth is at least $\beta n$
with high probability. The next statement says for which $\beta$ that happens.

\begin{theorem}[the admissible region]
\label{thm:betastar}
If $c\le4\ln2$ there is no $\beta\in(0,1)$ with $\delta(\beta,c)>0$. If
$c>4\ln2$ there is a number $\beta^{*}(c)\in(0,1)$ such that
$\delta(\beta,c)>0$ exactly when $\beta<\beta^{*}(c)$; it is the root in
$(0,1)$ of
\[
  -(1-\beta)\ln\tfrac{1-\beta}{2}-\beta\ln\beta \;=\; \tfrac{c}{4}(1-\beta)^{2}.
\]
Hence $\mathrm{pw}(G(n,c/n))\ge\beta n$ with probability $1-O(n^{3}e^{-\delta n})$
for every $\beta<\beta^{*}(c)$, and as $c\to\infty$,
$1-\beta^{*}(c)=\bigl(4\ln c-4\ln\ln c+O(1)\bigr)/c$.
\end{theorem}

Table~\ref{tab:betastar} gives $\beta^{*}(c)$ at a few values of $c$, beside the
only published explicit lower bound we know for a width parameter of
$G(n,c/n)$, the treewidth bound $1-\sqrt{9\ln3/(2c)}$ of
\citet{PerarnauSerra2014}, which is a lower bound on pathwidth too since
treewidth never exceeds pathwidth.

The threshold $4\ln2\approx2.77$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:betastar} belongs to the
method, not to the graphs: it is the point at which an edge-free split of the
vertices into two equal halves stops being likely, and below it the first
moment simply says nothing. The pathwidth of $G(n,c/n)$ is in fact linear in
$n$ for every $c>1$ \citep{LeeLeeOum2012}, with a constant that no one has
made explicit.

\subsection{The proof, in four steps}

\paragraph{Step 1: a narrow layout leaves an empty pair.}
Put the vertices in a line and look at any position $i$. The first $i+1$
vertices are the prefix; the rest are the suffix. A suffix vertex is
\emph{active} if it has a neighbour in the prefix. The vertex separation of
the line is the largest number of active vertices at any position, and by
Theorem~\ref{thm:vs} the smallest vertex separation over all lines is the
pathwidth. Now take a line of vertex separation $k$ and any position $i$. The
suffix has $n-i-1$ vertices, at most $k$ of them active, so at least $n-i-1-k$
suffix vertices have no neighbour at all in the prefix. Call that set $R$ and
the prefix $A$. Then $A$ and $R$ are disjoint, $|A|=i+1$, $|R|\ge n-i-1-k$, and
there is no edge between them: an \emph{empty pair}. This holds at every
position of every line, so a graph of pathwidth $k$ has an empty pair with
$|A|=t$ and $|R|\ge n-t-k$ for every $t\le n$. With $t=\lceil(n-k)/2\rceil$
both parts have about $(n-k)/2$ vertices~\leanref{emptypair}.

\paragraph{Step 2: count the graphs that have one.}
Fix a pair of disjoint sets $A$ and $R$ with $|A|=t$ and $|R|=t-1$. In
$G(n,p)$ the $t(t-1)$ possible edges between them are all absent with
probability $(1-p)^{t(t-1)}$. There are $\binom nt\binom{n-t}{t-1}$ such
pairs. A graph of pathwidth at most $k$ has at least one of them empty, so by
the union bound
\[
  \Pr[\mathrm{pw}\le k]\;\le\;\binom nt\binom{n-t}{t-1}\,(1-p)^{t(t-1)},
  \qquad t=\lceil(n-k)/2\rceil .
\]
This is the whole probabilistic content of the proof, and it is a Lean
theorem over the finite sum of the weights $p^{|E|}(1-p)^{\binom n2-|E|}$ of
all graphs on $n$ vertices, with no probability model needed; the count of
graphs with a given empty pair is exact there, $2^{\binom n2-t(t-1)}$ of
them~\leanref{firstmoment}.

\paragraph{Step 3: estimate the count.}
Two standard inequalities turn the right-hand side into an exponential in
$n$. The number of ways to choose the pair is the trinomial coefficient
$n!/\bigl(t!\,(t-1)!\,(n-2t+1)!\bigr)$, and a trinomial coefficient is at most
$\exp(nH)$ with $H$ the entropy of the three proportions $t/n$, $(t-1)/n$ and
$(n-2t+1)/n$ \citep[Theorem~11.1.3]{CoverThomas2006}: expand
$(x+y+z)^{n}=1$ at $x=t/n$, $y=(t-1)/n$, $z=(n-2t+1)/n$ and keep one term of
the sum. And $(1-p)^{m}\le e^{-pm}$ because $1-p\le e^{-p}$. With $p=c/n$ the
second factor is at most $\exp(-c\,t(t-1)/n)$.

\paragraph{Step 4: the bookkeeping.}
The three proportions are not exactly $a$, $a$ and $1-2a$: with
$k=\lfloor\beta n\rfloor$, each is within $1/n$ of its target. A shift of $d$
in one argument of $\phi$ changes $\phi$ by at most $\phi(d)$ when $d\le1/e$,
and $\phi(1/n)=(\ln n)/n$; three shifts cost $3\ln n$ in the exponent, which
is the $3\ln n$ of the theorem and the reason for $n\ge3$. Likewise
$t(t-1)\ge a^{2}n^{2}-an$, and the $-an$ costs the term $c\,a$. Multiplying
the two estimates of Step~3 into the bound of Step~2 gives
$\exp(nH(a,a,1-2a)-ca^{2}n+3\ln n+ca)$, which is Theorem~\ref{thm:firstmoment};
$a\le1/2$ gives its second form. Nothing is hidden in an error term: the
inequalities of Step~3 are exact bounds on the two factors, and the two
corrections are the ones just named~\leanref{firstmomentA}.

\paragraph{The admissible region.}
On $(0,\tfrac12]$ the function $g(a)=H(a,a,1-2a)-ca^{2}$ is strictly concave,
tends to $0$ as $a\to0$, and equals $\ln2-c/4$ at $a=\tfrac12$. If
$c\le4\ln2$ it is nonnegative throughout, so $\delta=-g$ is never positive.
If $c>4\ln2$ it has exactly one zero $a^{*}$ in $(0,\tfrac12)$, is positive
before it and negative after it, so $\delta(\beta,c)>0$ exactly when
$a=(1-\beta)/2>a^{*}$, that is when $\beta<1-2a^{*}=:\beta^{*}(c)$.
Substituting $a=(1-\beta)/2$ into $g(a)=0$ gives the equation of
Theorem~\ref{thm:betastar}, and the asymptotic form follows from the
two-sided estimate $2\ln(1/a)-ca\le g(a)/a\le2\ln(1/a)+2-ca$. This part is
proved on paper only.

\subsection{The benchmark generator}
\label{app:random-cs}

The random instances of Section~\ref{sec:results} are not $G(n,p)$. The
generator of \citet{ChuStuckey2009} fills a customer-by-pattern table with
independent entries at a density chosen to give $d$ customers per pattern on
average, and discards any instance whose MOSP graph is disconnected; the
files show two further rules the paper does not state, every customer given
at least one pattern and every pattern at least two customers, and with those
rules a simulated generator reproduces the size statistics of all forty
classes. The MOSP graph of such an instance is the union of one clique per
pattern. Steps 1 and 2 of the proof survive this change unaltered: a graph of
pathwidth $k$ still has an empty pair, and a pair is empty exactly when no
pattern contains a customer of each side. If the patterns are independent
random customer sets of sizes $s_1,\dots,s_m$, the probability that a given
pair $(A,R)$ with $|A|=t$, $|R|=t-1$ is empty factorises over the patterns,
and the union bound gives
\[
  \Pr[\mathrm{pw}\le k]\;\le\;\binom nt\binom{n-t}{t-1}\prod_{j=1}^{m}
  \frac{\binom{n-t}{s_j}+\binom{n-t+1}{s_j}-\binom{n-2t+1}{s_j}}{\binom{n}{s_j}},
\]
the fraction being the share of $s_j$-sets that miss $A$ or miss $R$. This is
a Lean theorem in the paper's own vocabulary, with the instance built from the
patterns and the event stated as $\mathrm{MOSP}\le k+1$~\leanref{clique-cover}.
It is computable from the pattern sizes alone, so it predicts the benchmark
from the generator's parameters. Table~\ref{tab:cs-floor} gives, for each of
the forty classes, the mean certified optimum of its five instances beside
the floor the inequality gives at confidence $0.01$, that is, the largest
value it does not rule out at that level plus one. None of the 200 certified
optima lies below its floor. At $d=10$ the floor is within $5\%$ of the
optimum, at $d=4$ within $20\%$; at $d=2$ it is a third short, and
asymptotically it says nothing there: with $m=\mu n$ patterns of $d$
customers, the exponent of the expected number of balanced empty pairs is
$\ln2\,\bigl(1+\mu(1-d)\bigr)$, so the method gives a positive constant
exactly when $\mu(d-1)>1$. That quantity is the mean number of patterns per
customer minus one, the number of ones in the table minus the number of
patterns, per customer; call it the excess. The classes of
Section~\ref{sec:results} that took thousands of core-hours to certify, the
sparsest ones ($d=2$), have excess between $1.66$ and $1.78$, just inside the
region where this argument proves linear pathwidth; on generated random covers
the cost of the exact search peaks at excess about $2$ to $2.4$, between the
$d=2$ classes and the $d=4$ classes (excess $2.9$ to $3.4$ at 125 customers).
Section~\ref{app:excess} places every instance on that axis and tests, and
rejects, the hypothesis that the peak is where this bound is loosest.

The inequality assumes the patterns are uniform given their sizes, which the
padding and the rejection break. What is proved is the inequality with the
sizes the table has \emph{before} the repairs. On the Bernoulli table a given
pair is empty with probability exactly
$[(1-p)^t+(1-p)^{t-1}-(1-p)^{2t-1}]^m$, the binomial average of the fraction
above; the repairs only add ones, so an empty pair of the padded table is an
empty pair of the original~\leanref{coupling} and the bound with Bernoulli
sizes holds for the padded instance; and if the patterns are padded before the customers, the
rows after that pad are independent uniform sets of size $\max(s,2)$ with $s$
binomial, and the bound holds with those sizes. The rejection is not an
increasing event of the table entries: adding an entry can make a pad vanish,
disconnect the instance and lower its pathwidth, so Harris's inequality does
not apply to it, and conditioning on acceptance costs at most the factor
$1/\Pr[\text{accepted}]$, which is measured: within $1\%$ of $1$ at
$d\ge6$ and on the square classes at $d=4$, $0.77$ at the $100\times50$,
$d=4$ class, $0.53$ to $0.87$ at $d=2$, and $0.005$ at the $100\times50$,
$d=2$ class. A Chernoff bound on the pattern sizes gives a quenched form of
the same floor, two to four stacks higher on the largest classes. These
proved floors lie below the floors of Table~\ref{tab:cs-floor}, by zero to
five stacks at $d\ge8$, by two to twelve at $d=4$ and $6$ on the square
classes and by more on the $100\times50$ classes; at $d=2$ they are trivial,
because $40\%$ of the Bernoulli patterns have at most one customer and the
pads that make them count are exactly what the proof cannot use. What
remains between the proved floors and the table is, at $d\ge4$, the
difference between a bound on the average over size vectors and the typical
size vector, and at $d=2$ the pads. With the sizes the files actually have
the statement is measured, not proved: under the fitted generator the repairs and the
rejection make empty pairs rarer than the uniform model says, by a factor
that falls from $1.0$ to $0.6$ as the sets grow, and on 7{,}200 fresh
instances at 30 and 40 customers solved exactly none has an optimum below its
$0.01$ floor.

The same first moment appears in \citet{Gao2012} for random intersection
graphs, with treewidth in place of pathwidth. His proof drops the factor
corresponding to the product above between two lines, which is harmless when
the number of patterns is sublinear in $n$ and changes the constant when it is
linear; his threshold of two patterns per customer is the dropped factor's
artefact, and with it restored the condition at $m=n$ is about $2.46$.

\subsection{The excess, and a phase diagram}
\label{app:excess}

The quantity that ends the previous subsection deserves a name and an axis.
For an instance with $n$ customers, $m$ patterns and $N$ ones in the table,
the \emph{excess} is $e=(N-m)/n$, the mean number of patterns per customer
minus one. With $r=m/n$ and $c=N/m$ the mean customers per pattern,
$e=r(c-1)$; and if the customer--pattern incidence graph $B$, with a node per
customer and per pattern and an edge per one in the table, has $\kappa$
components, it has $n(e-1)+\kappa$ independent cycles, so $B$ can be a forest
only when $e<1$. We read $e$ from the table, never from a generator's nominal
parameters, whose repairs inflate it. Three values of $e$ carry a meaning.
The constant $\beta^{*}(d,\mu)$ is positive exactly when $\mu(d-1)>1$, that
is when $e>1$ (on paper, from the exponent of~\leanref{clique-cover}). On
random covers of 15 to 75 customers at every ratio $m/n$ from $1/8$ to $2$,
the nodes our search spends to refute $\mathrm{optimum}-1$ peak at $e=2$ to
$2.4$, falling by two orders of magnitude on either side (measured). And at
$e_g(r)=(\sqrt{r^{2}+4r}-r)/2$, $0.62$ for $m=n$, the branching factor of the
random incidence graph is one: on generated covers of 10 to 75 customers the
share of instances whose $B$ is a forest falls from $0.74$--$0.92$ below
$e_g(r)$ to $0.23$--$0.40$ between $e_g(r)$ and $1$, and is zero above
(measured). Figure~\ref{fig:excess} places every instance of
Section~\ref{sec:results} against the three. Below $e_g(r)$ the open stacks
are governed by the largest pattern (Theorem~\ref{thm:blowup}); between
$e_g(r)$ and $1$ the first moment says nothing and the deterministic bounds
are all there is; between $1$ and the ridge the certified optimum per
customer sits $0.02$--$0.10$ above $\beta^{*}$ and the cost of certification
climbs, and this is where the $d=2$ classes of Section~\ref{sec:results}
($e=1.66$--$1.78$) and 22 of the 24 industrial instances sit; above the ridge
the cost falls and $\beta^{*}$ comes within $0.04$--$0.06$ of the certified
slope at $e\ge6$ ($m=n$, 30--100 customers), the $d=4$ classes
($e=2.9$--$3.7$) being the dense shoulder. No benchmark instance we hold has
$e<e_g(r)$, and 12 of $6{,}376$ have $e<1$.

\paragraph{The forest regime.}
Write $\omega$ for the size of the largest pattern.
\begin{theorem}[blow-up]
\label{thm:blowup}
For every instance, $\mathrm{MOSP}\le\omega\,(\pw(B)+1)$. If $B$ is a forest,
$\mathrm{MOSP}\le\omega\bigl(\lfloor\log_{3}\tfrac{6(n+m)+3}{5}\rfloor+1\bigr)$.
\end{theorem}
Replace each pattern node in each bag of a path decomposition of $B$ by the
pattern's customers: a customer's nodes in $B$ form a star, so its bags stay
an interval, and no bag grows by more than a factor $\omega$. Nothing about
$B$ is used; the forest enters only through the pathwidth of a forest on $N$
vertices, at most $\lfloor\log_{3}((6N+3)/5)\rfloor$, which is sharp. Both are
Lean theorems~\leanref{blowup}~\leanref{forest}. The bound is loose: on 7,004
graphs with exact pathwidth (clique trees to 936 vertices, random block trees,
3,448 certified random covers whose $B$ is a forest, and 2,000 small random
instances) it is never violated and never exact, 2 to 5.5 times the truth on
the block-graph families; on the 3,448 random forests the pathwidth is
$\omega-1$ on $99.8\%$, so below $e_g(r)$ the optimum is, in practice, the
largest pattern (measured). The exact theory on block graphs is polynomial
\citep{ChouKoHoChen2008}; on clique trees the exact values grow by about
$(\omega-2)/2$ per level of depth, no formula linear in $\omega$ and the depth
fits them, and we conjecture no closed form.

\begin{lemma}[pendant cliques]
\label{lem:pendant}
If a clique $R$ has $q\ge1$ cliques of size at least $t$ pendant at distinct
vertices of $R$, each meeting $R$ in its attachment vertex only, then
$\pw\ge t+\lceil q/2\rceil-2$.
\end{lemma}
Every clique lies inside one bag; if a pendant's bag is the bag of $R$ the two
share a bag of size $|R|+t-1$, and otherwise at least $\lceil q/2\rceil$
pendants have their bags on one side of $R$'s, and the one nearest $R$ holds
its own clique together with the attachment vertices of the others, by the
interval property. It is a Lean theorem~\leanref{pendant}, and with
$t=q=|R|$ it gives the exact value $\lceil 3s/2\rceil-2$ of a clique $K_s$
with a pendant $K_s$ at every vertex, the depth-one clique tree; with the
matching decomposition (on paper), $K_s$ with a pendant $K_t$ at $q$ of its
vertices has pathwidth exactly $\max(s-1,\,t+\lceil q/2\rceil-2)$. It is a
pathwidth argument that is not degree-based, and on the certified instances
it is worth little: evaluated over the maximal cliques of the MOSP graph it is
above the clique floor $\omega-1$ on 554 of the $6{,}376$ and tight on
$44\%$, but above the solver's bound on 5, and above $\max(\text{solver
bound},\,\mathrm{tw}+1)$ on 3 of the 338 instances where that bound misses by
two or more, by one stack each (measured, 9--134 customers).

\paragraph{Constructions from above.}
\begin{lemma}[layers]
\label{lem:layer}
If $L_0,L_1,\dots$ are the distance layers from any vertex of a connected
graph, $\pw\le\max_i(|L_i|+|L_{i+1}|)-1$; and $\pw\le n-\alpha$ for any
independent set of $\alpha$ vertices.
\end{lemma}
The bags are $L_i\cup L_{i+1}$, and $(V\setminus I)\cup\{v\}$ for $v\in
I$~\leanref{layer}~\leanref{indep}. On the uniform cover model with $m=\mu n$
patterns of size $d$ the layer sizes follow a two-type exploration recursion
and the greedy independent set that takes the customer with fewest patterns
follows a differential equation; the smaller limit is a constant
$\beta^{+}(d,\mu)$, derived on paper up to the concentration step, which we
cite and do not re-prove. Table~\ref{tab:excess} gives it beside $\beta^{*}$
and the slope $b$ of $\mathrm{opt}=a+bn$ on the certified instances at
30--100 customers. The sandwich
$\beta^{*}\le\liminf\mathrm{opt}/n\le\limsup\mathrm{opt}/n\le\beta^{+}$ has
width below $0.12$ at $e\ge14$ and $0.42$--$0.49$ for $1\le e\le3.5$: it says
least where the search works hardest. The best published upper bound we know,
the degree-sequence bound of \citet[Corollary~5.2]{EdwardsMcDermid2015}, is
wider still on the cover degree law, $0.66$, $0.86$ and $0.96$ at $d=4$, $6$,
$10$ with $\mu=1$, because it sees the degrees of a union of cliques and not
the cliques. The greedy closing rule we use for
upper bounds cannot be analysed this way; at 5,000 customers its width per
customer is $0.06$--$0.36$ below $\beta^{+}$ (measured, no exact solve).

\paragraph{Why the first moment is loose.}
A layout of width $k$ forces $\ell$ nested empty pairs, one per prefix size,
but counting them gives a constant \emph{below} $\beta^{*}$ on every cell (by
$0.051$ at $\ell=2$, $d=6$, $\mu=1$, and more for larger $\ell$, at every
spacing): naming $\varepsilon n$ more vertices costs
$\Theta(\varepsilon\log(1/\varepsilon))$ of entropy and buys
$\Theta(\varepsilon)$ of constraint. The slack is in the union bound. The
other direction is a count that names \emph{fewer} vertices, and we tried
three: the event itself, counted once per separator; the closed pair; and a
weighted count. A layout of width $k<n$ forces a set $S$ of $k$ vertices whose
removal leaves the other $N=n-k$ vertices split into two sides of sizes
$\lceil N/2\rceil$ and $\lfloor N/2\rfloor$ with no edge between them, and
for a fixed $S$ the probability of that event depends only on the component
structure of $G-S$, which is $G(N,p)$.

\begin{theorem}[separators, finite form]
\label{thm:separator}
For $0\le p\le1$ and $k<n$,
$\Pr[\pw(G(n,p))\le k]\le\binom nk\,P_N(E_{\mathrm{bip}})$, where
$P_N(E_{\mathrm{bip}})$ is the probability that the components of $G(N,p)$ can
be grouped into two classes of sizes $\lceil N/2\rceil$ and $\lfloor N/2\rfloor$.
\end{theorem}

The union bound over the $\binom nk$ sets $S$ is the only inequality: the
graphs on $n$ vertices with a given restriction to $V\setminus S$ have total
weight equal to the restriction's $G(N,p)$ weight, and the inner probability
is the same for every $S$. It is a Lean theorem with the inner probability as a
finite weighted count~\leanref{theoremS}; on paper that count is a sum over the
integer partitions of $N$ with Gilbert's connectedness recursion, which we
evaluate exactly to $N=120$. Step~2 bounds the same event by its number of
witnesses, $\binom Nt(1-p)^{t(N-t)}$; the theorem merges every balanced empty
bipartition of $G-S$ into one event. Writing $c'=c(1-\beta)$ for the mean
degree of $G-S$ and $I(c')$ for the rate at which the probability that no
component of $G(N,c'/N)$ exceeds $N/2$ decays, the constant becomes the root
$\beta_{\mathrm{sep}}(c)$ of $H(\beta)=(1-\beta)\,I(c(1-\beta))$. That rate is a corollary of the
large-deviation principle for the largest component
\citep{OConnell1998,Puhalskii2005,AndreisKonigPatterson2021}, whose rate
function at one half is positive exactly when $c'>2\ln2$; our exact inner
probabilities reproduce the quoted rate to $0.002$ at seven values of $c'$
(fitted over $N=100$--$400$), and the bipartition event has the same rate as
its relaxation to $N=120$. With it, $\beta_{\mathrm{sep}}=0.037$ at $c=3$
against $\beta^{*}=0.009$, and $0.341$ at $c=10$ against $0.337$: a third of
the gap to the slope fitted to exact pathwidths ($0.099$) at $c=3$, a
twentieth at $c=10$ ($0.415$). Conditional on the cited large-deviation upper
bound, the threshold of the method moves from $4\ln2$ to $2\ln2$, the density
at which the giant component passes half the vertices and the bisection width
becomes linear \citep{LuczakMcDiarmid2001}; the true threshold is $1$. The
separator as the object of a width first moment is in \citet{DoErdeKang2024},
with a deterministic bound on the bipartitions per separator in place of their
probability, and the exact inner count of a single vertex cut of a random
regular graph in \citet{KolesnikWormald2014}; we found no prior statement of
the inner probability of the bipartition event, nor of its use for a width
constant.

The closed pair, in which every separator vertex is adjacent to both sides
($S=N(A)\cap N(R)$; a layout forces one with sides at least the forced sizes),
is the dense-side counterpart. Its expected number is exactly
$\sum n!/(a!\,r!\,s!)\,(1-p)^{ar}\bigl((1-(1-p)^{a})(1-(1-p)^{r})\bigr)^{s}$ over
the admissible size triples (the union bound and the characterisation in
Lean~\leanref{theoremCl}, the independence step on paper), and its constant
exceeds $\beta^{*}$ by $0.007$--$0.010$ at $c=6$--$12$, and by $0.011$ on random
covers with $d=4$ and $d=6$, $\mu=1$, where the separator count has no
large-deviation rate to cite. A weighted count, each empty pair divided by the
number of pairs reachable from it by moving low-degree separator vertices
across, is valid too~\leanref{weightedcount} and adds nothing to the closed
pair on this record, its lower tail being the event that the pair is closed.
None of the three moves the headline constants by the $0.02$ we had set as
worth a page; at $c=6$ and $10$ the best of them closes $12\%$ and $11\%$ of
the gap to the fitted slope.

What the cluster is we measured directly, by planting an empty pair and
enumerating the others ($n\le32$ on $G(n,c/n)$ at $c=6$ and $10$, $n\le28$ on
covers at $d=6$, $\mu=1$, 200 instances per cell). The other empty pairs form
two lobes, perturbations of the planted pair and of its reverse through the
separator, with nothing between; on the covers the median planted instance
has no pair but the reverse, so the $e^{0.23n}$ is carried by rare instances,
and on $G(n,c/n)$ the median cluster grows at about half the rate of the mean.
The whole slack of the union bound at a cell is the mean of $1/X$ under the
planted law, a bounded statistic: $0.065$, $0.039$ and $0.043$ nats per vertex
at the largest sizes, $0.019$, $0.012$ and $0.015$ in $\beta$, sub-linear in
$n$ over the range (measured). In stacks, on certified instances, the counts
of this paragraph move the floor by one to two at 60 to 75 vertices and the
pathwidth sits four to five higher; Harper's vertex-isoperimetric bound, a
deterministic consequence of the empty pair, is within a stack of the
pathwidth wherever our branch and bound reaches the forced prefix, which puts
at least three quarters of the leftover in the union bound as read on
instances, where the planted measurement at $n\le32$ reads $0.012$--$0.019$ in
$\beta$; the two instruments see different sizes, and we do not reconcile
them.

\paragraph{One parameter, or two.}
The excess sets the threshold, the ridge and the scale, not the value. At
$e=2$ the certified slope is $0.282$, $0.232$, $0.200$, $0.167$ for
$\mu=1/4,1/2,1,2$ (standard errors $0.002$--$0.006$), and 13 of 16 pairs of
series matched in $e$ to $\pm0.05$ differ by more than twice their error;
$\beta^{*}$ has the same dependence ($0.180$, $0.169$, $0.147$, $0.123$), and
over 41 series $b=0.057+0.984\,\beta^{*}$ with residual $0.024$. Nor is the
ridge where the first moment is loosest: on 25 series at 50--75 customers
the gap between the certified optimum and the floor peaks at $e=2.2$--$5.3$,
on the dense side, with the ridge on a plateau within one stack of the
maximum. What peaks on the ridge is the number of closed sets the search can
reach, not the slack of the bound.

\paragraph{Twenty-four industrial instances.}
The 24 SCOOP instances \citep{Frinhani2018} have excess $0.97$ to $2.50$,
median $1.35$; 19 lie in $[1,2)$, none has a forest $B$, ten decompose. Every
refutation on record for them took at most $22{,}433$ nodes, and the greedy
closing rule is exact on 12 and at most $0.061n$ above the optimum. One, with
60 customers, 49 patterns and optimum 7, sits two stacks below the floor a
random cover with its pattern sizes has at confidence $0.01$: no
contradiction, the floor being a random-model statement, but a measure of
distance from random, on which the designed Challenge instances sit up to 39
stacks below. These are 24 instances from one source, read with pieces as
customers (the orientation of the files' format line, the opposite of their
description's prose); we say nothing about industry beyond them.

\subsection{What is checked, and what is known}

Steps 1, 2, 3 and 4 and the two inequalities of Theorem~\ref{thm:firstmoment}
are Lean theorems with the statement exactly as printed, including the
constant~3; so is the consequence that the probability tends to zero whenever
$\delta(\beta,c)>0$. The entropy bound on trinomial coefficients is not in
Mathlib and is proved there from the binomial theorem. Theorem~\ref{thm:betastar}
is proved on paper, and the values in Table~\ref{tab:betastar} were computed
by two programs written independently. Theorem~\ref{thm:separator} is a Lean
theorem with its inner probability as a finite weighted count; of the
closed-pair count, the union bound and the characterisation of a closed pair
are in Lean and the independence step is on paper; the constants
$\beta_{\mathrm{sep}}$ rest on a cited large-deviation rate, and every rate we
fitted is called fitted.

The argument is published twice in cruder form. \citet{Turner1986} used the
same empty pair, with the count $(en/k)^{2k}$ in place of the entropy, to
prove that almost every $G(n,p)$ with $p$ fixed has bandwidth at least
$n-4\log_{1/(1-p)}n$; since bandwidth is at least pathwidth, our dense
statement implies his and not conversely. \citet{DiazPetitSernaTrevisan2001}
used it for vertex separation on mixing graphs, with a $3^{n}$ count and
Chernoff slack at a single position, which gives no positive constant below
$c\approx227$. Neither states the entropy form, a value of $\beta^{*}(c)$, the
threshold $4\ln2$ or the asymptotics, and we found no later work that does.

One computation says how far the finite inequality is from the truth. On
133 random graphs with 60 vertices and $c=10$ whose pathwidth we computed
exactly, the smallest pathwidth is 25, while Theorem~\ref{thm:firstmoment}'s
first inequality, evaluated exactly rather than through Step~3, puts the
probability of pathwidth at most 23 below $0.35$ and of at most 21 below
$10^{-3}$. The gap between $\beta^{*}(c)$ and the slopes fitted to such data
is of the order of $0.1$ and does not close with $n$. Counting the event a
narrow layout forces instead of its witnesses, once per separator or once per
closed pair (the paragraph \emph{Why the first moment is loose} in
Appendix~\ref{app:excess}), closes a third of it at $c=3$ and a tenth at $c=6$
and $10$; the best published upper bound, the degree-sequence bound of
\citet[Corollary~5.2]{EdwardsMcDermid2015}, gives $0.183$, $0.458$ and $0.656$
on Poisson degrees at $c=3$, $6$ and $10$, so the provable sandwich on
$\pw/n$ is $[0.037,0.183]$, $[0.182,0.458]$ and $[0.345,0.656]$ there, against
fitted slopes of $0.099$, $0.268$ and $0.415$.
