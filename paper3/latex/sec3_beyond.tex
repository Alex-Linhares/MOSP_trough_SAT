\section{What the manuscript claims beyond the verified statement}
\label{sec:beyond}

The manuscript \citep{OAI:The-Exponential-Time-Hypothesis-is-False-October-25-2026}
is 67 pages long. The verified statement is 220 lines long. Section~\ref{sec:statement}
read the statement. This section reads the manuscript's claims one by
one. For each claim it says one of three things: this is what the Lean
theorem asserts; this follows from the Lean theorem by an argument that
exists only on paper; or this rests on a bound that the Lean library
does not contain at all.

The collection draws the line itself, in a short note on the family.
We quote its two sentences in full (\clonefile{lean/docs/378.md}, lines
9 and 11):
\begin{quote}\small\sloppy
``For fixed $k \ge 2$, Lean proves that a deterministic machine exactly
counts satisfying assignments of a canonically encoded $k$-CNF on $n$
variables in time $(L+1)^{O_k(1)}\,2^{O_k(n(\log\log n)^2/\sqrt{\log n})}$,
where $L$ is the encoding's bit length. At every step, the machine has
visited at most $(L+1)^{O_k(1)}$ tape cells in total.''

``For $m$ clauses, the October 25 manuscript gives the sharper time bound
$(n+m)^{O_k(1)}\,2^{O_k(n\log\log n/\sqrt{\log n})}$. When $m \le Dn$ for
fixed $D > 0$, this improves to
$(n+m)^{O_{k,D}(1)}\,2^{O_{k,D}(n/\sqrt{\log n})}$.''
\end{quote}
In words (the notation $O_k(1)$ means a constant that depends only on $k$, Section~\ref{sec:statement}): the Lean proves the bound with the exponent of Section~\ref{sec:statement}, the one with the \emph{squared} double logarithm. The manuscript claims two better exponents, one with the
double logarithm not squared, and one, for formulas with few clauses,
with no double logarithm at all. Everything below is an elaboration of
that note against the manuscript's text and the library's files.
Table~\ref{tab:beyond-claims} is the summary.

\subsection{Three statuses, and what ``paper-only'' means}
\label{sec:beyond-statuses}

We tag every claim with one of three words.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Verified.} The claim is the comparator statement
  \texttt{exact\_count\_and\_sat}, or one of its parts, or a theorem of
  the solution tree that the replay of Section~\ref{sec:replay} compiled
  and whose axioms it printed. Nothing else is called verified.
\item \textbf{Supported.} The claim follows from the verified statement
  by an argument that is on paper only: a short reduction, or a
  citation. The argument is not in the Lean library, and we have not
  checked it. What we have checked is narrower: the only property of
  the counter the argument uses is one the verified statement provides,
  and the exponent it inherits is therefore $\rhon$ and not a sharper
  one.
\item \textbf{Paper-only.} The claim rests on a bound that the Lean
  library at commit \texttt{4eaee875} does not contain: Theorem~1.1's
  two sharper exponents, and whatever is derived from them.
\end{itemize}

Our working plan for this section had two tags, verified and paper-only. The
middle one is ours. The manuscript's corollaries are of two different
kinds, and one word would have put Corollary~1.3, an encoding into the
verified counter, beside Corollary~1.4, a bound whose proof calls the
sharper exponent by name. In that two-way split, ``supported'' is
a kind of ``paper-only'': not in the library. In no case does
``paper-only'' mean ``wrong''. We did not read the manuscript's proofs
for correctness. The tag records where a claim is established, not
whether it is.

\subsection{The sharper exponents of Theorem~1.1}
\label{sec:beyond-exponents}

Theorem~1.1 (p.~2, lines 56--62) states two running-time bounds for the
same algorithm. The first holds for every $k$-CNF on $n \ge 20$
variables: a polynomial in $n + m$ times
$2^{O_k(n\log\log n/\sqrt{\log n})}$. The second holds when the number of
clauses is at most a fixed multiple $D$ of $n$: a polynomial times
$2^{O_{k,D}(n/\sqrt{\log n})}$. Both use a polynomial amount of space
(line 60). The sentence that follows the theorem is the one this paper's
title is about (p.~2, lines 63--67):
\begin{quote}\small
``The rational interpolation argument in Section 6.4 gives the weaker
exponent $O_k(\rho(n))$, where
$\rho(n) = n(\log\log n)^2/\sqrt{\log n} = n/(\log n)^{1/2+o(1)}$.
Appendix D proves both bounds in Theorem 1.1 by tensor contraction.''
\end{quote}
Proposition~6.4 (p.~43, lines 2474--2476) states the weaker bound on its
own: for each fixed $k \ge 2$, the interpolation algorithm counts in a
polynomial times $2^{O_k(\rho(n))}$ bit operations and polynomial space,
for $n \ge 20$.

\paragraph{Which of the three is in the Lean.}
The verified exponent is the weaker one. The comparator's
\runtimeScale{} (lines 132--134 of the comparator file) is
$n(\ln\ln(n+16))^2/\sqrt{\ln(n+16)}$, the squared form. The solution's
own exponent, called \texttt{rho}
(\clonefile{SqrtLogSpace/Asymptotics.lean}, lines 10--11), is the same
function with base-2 logarithms, and a lemma,
\path{runtimeScale_le_four_rho} (lines 190--191), bounds the
comparator's exponent by four times it for $n \ge 20$. The proof is built around the parameters of the manuscript's
Section~6.4 to the letter. Those are the three numbers that set how the
proof thins the formula before counting (its ``sparsification''). The
manuscript selects $\lambda_n = \lceil\log_2 n\rceil$,
$\nu_n = \lceil\log_2\lambda_n\rceil$ and
$s = \lfloor\sqrt{\lambda_n}/\nu_n^2\rfloor$, ``the largest nonnegative
integer satisfying $s^2\nu_n^4 \le \lambda_n$'' (p.~43, lines
2480--2486). Here $\lceil x\rceil$ is the smallest whole number at least
$x$ and $\lfloor x\rfloor$ the largest at most $x$. And
\path{Asymptotics.lean} lines 14--20 define \texttt{sparsifyLambda},
\texttt{sparsifyNu} and \texttt{sparsifySchedule} as exactly those three
quantities. What
Appendix~D of the manuscript proves is not in the library in any form we
could find (Section~\ref{sec:beyond-lean}). So the general exponent
$n\log\log n/\sqrt{\log n}$ and the fixed-density exponent
$n/\sqrt{\log n}$ are \textbf{paper-only}: proved at p.~57 (lines
3222--3246) and p.~54 (lines 3045--3064) respectively, by the
contraction theorem of Appendix~D.

\paragraph{Both bounds from one algorithm.}
The manuscript notes that a single algorithm attains both paper-only
bounds, by switching between two methods according to the number of
clauses (p.~57, lines 3247--3250). That algorithm is not the verified one
either.

\paragraph{The three exponents, in numbers.}
Table~\ref{tab:beyond-exponents} puts the three exponents side by side,
each divided by $n$ and each with its constant taken to be $1$. A value
above $1$ means the exponent is above $n$, so the bound is worse than
trying every assignment. The verified ratio is the one of
Table~\ref{tab:runtime-scale}. The general paper-only ratio,
$\ln\ln n/\sqrt{\ln n}$, never reaches $1$: it peaks at $2/e = 0.736$
at $n \approx 1{,}618$ and falls on both sides. The fixed-density ratio
$1/\sqrt{\ln n}$ falls from $0.578$ at $n = 20$. So, at constant $1$,
both paper-only guarantees sit below $2^n$ at every size, and the
verified one sits above $2^n$ from $n = 50$ to $n \approx 10^{2390}$.
That is the whole content of the word ``verified'' in the title: the
manuscript's exponents would make a different sentence, and they are
not what the kernel checked. Two cautions apply to the table equally.
The constants in front of every exponent are not computed anywhere, in
the manuscript or in the Lean. And the manuscript's exponents are
negative below $n = e$ (and undefined at $n \le 1$) and are stated for $n \ge 20$, while the
verified one is stated for all $n$. Figure~\ref{fig:central} draws the
same three curves.

\paragraph{The abstract's form.}
The manuscript's abstract states the running time as a polynomial times
$2^{n/(\log n)^{1/2+o(1)}}$ (p.~1, lines 6--9). The small term $o(1)$ in
that exponent is a quantity that goes to zero as $n$ grows. The verified exponent is of that form too, because the square of a double logarithm grows more slowly than any fixed power of the logarithm (in symbols, $(\ln\ln n)^2$ is $(\ln n)^{o(1)}$), and the manuscript says so at lines 64--66. We tag the
abstract's form \textbf{verified}, with the remark that its $o(1)$
hides exactly the factor by which the three rows of
Table~\ref{tab:beyond-exponents} differ: at $n = 10^6$ that factor is
$6.9$ for the verified exponent, $2.6$ for the general one and $1$ for
the fixed-density one.

\subsection{The corollaries}
\label{sec:beyond-corollaries}

The corollaries speak of objects from the manuscript's own fields:
circuits, graphs, maps. We gloss each in a clause where it first appears
and define none further; a reader who needs more should go to the
manuscript at the lines cited.
\paragraph{Finding and sampling a satisfying assignment (p.~2, lines 68--71).}
``When $\#F > 0$, the standard self-reduction finds a satisfying
assignment by fixing one variable at a time and checking that the
remaining count is positive. Choosing each bit according to the two
conditional counts instead gives an exactly uniform random satisfying
assignment,'' with polynomial expected overhead and a citation.
\textbf{Supported}. The method calls the verified counter at most $n$
times on formulas of the same width, and the verified statement
provides the exact count it needs. The method is two sentences of prose
and is not in the library.

\paragraph{Corollary 1.2, Max-$k$-SAT within $\varepsilon m$ (p.~2, lines 77--94).}
A randomised algorithm finds an assignment that satisfies at least
$\mathrm{OPT}(F) - \varepsilon m$ clauses, that is, within a small
fraction of the best possible, with high probability, in time
\[
  (n+m)^{O_{k,\varepsilon}(1)}\,2^{O_{k,\varepsilon}(\rho(n))}
\]
(line 80). The corollary is stated with $\rho(n)$ itself, the verified
exponent. Its proof samples $O(n/\varepsilon^2)$ clauses and builds a small circuit (a network of logic gates). It turns the circuit into a 3-CNF with
$O_{k,\varepsilon}(n)$ variables by a cited lemma. Then it calls Theorem~1.1 repeatedly, narrowing down the best value and then fixing variables one at a time (lines 82--94).
\textbf{Supported}: the exponent matches the verified one because
$\rho(cn) = O_c(\rho(n))$, and every call is to a fixed-width counter on
$O(n)$ variables; the sampling, the circuit and the reduction are on
paper. Its randomness is in the reduction, not in the counter.

\paragraph{Corollary 1.3, independent sets, colourings, perfect matchings (p.~2--3, lines 100--120).}
For a graph on $v$ vertices and $e$ edges, three counts. The number of
independent sets (sets of vertices no two of which are joined by an
edge), in time $2^{o(v)}$. For fixed $q$, the number of proper
$q$-colourings (colourings with $q$ colours such that no edge joins two
vertices of the same colour), in time $2^{o(v)}$. And the number of
perfect matchings (sets of edges touching every vertex exactly once), in
time $2^{o(v+e)}$. All three in polynomial space. The encodings are
explicit. For independent sets: one variable per vertex and a clause of
two literals per edge (lines 107--112). For colourings: $q$ variables per
vertex with ``exactly one colour'' and ``no equal colours'' clauses, a
$q$-CNF on $qv$ variables (lines 112--115). For matchings: one variable
per edge and a linear-size circuit for ``exactly one selected edge meets
each vertex'', reduced to 3-CNF with $O(v+e)$ variables, with the extra
variables fixed by the edge bits so that the count is preserved (lines
116--119). \textbf{Supported}: the conclusion is
$2^{o(\cdot)}$, which needs only that $\rhon$ grows more slowly than $n$
\leanref{rho-little-o}, and each encoding preserves the count into a
fixed-width CNF on $O(v)$ or $O(v+e)$ variables. None of the three
encodings is in the library.

\paragraph{Corollary 1.4, a circuit lower bound for $\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{NP}}$ (p.~3, lines 121--127).}
``The fixed-density case of Theorem 1.1 gives a circuit lower bound for
$\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{NP}}$'' (line 121). A circuit lower bound is a proof that some problem needs large circuits; $\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{NP}}$ is a class of problems whose definition is not needed here. The claim: there is a problem $L$ in that class and a constant $c > 0$ such that $L$ needs circuits of more than $c\,n\sqrt{\log n}$ gates for infinitely many $n$ (lines 123--127). The proof is Appendix~C.1 (p.~51--52, lines 2925--2967), an argument of
Williams's. It invokes the paper-only exponent in so many words: ``Put
$a(t) = \sqrt{\log_2 t}$. Testing whether the count in the
constant-density part of Theorem 1.1 is positive decides 3-SAT with $V$
variables and at most $5V$ clauses in time $V^b\,2^{AV/a(V)}$ for fixed
$A, b > 0$'' (p.~52, lines 2949--2950). The threshold
$s_n = \lfloor\eta n\lfloor a(n)\rfloor\rfloor$ is chosen against that
$a$ (line 2953), and the constant $c$ comes out as
$\sqrt{\eta/(d\log 2)}$ (line 2967). \textbf{Paper-only}: the
quantitative bound $c\,n\sqrt{\log n}$ is tied to $2^{AV/\sqrt{\log V}}$,
which the library does not contain. What the same argument would give
from the verified exponent is a question about the manuscript's proof,
which we do not examine (Section~\ref{sec:beyond-not}).

\paragraph{Corollary C.1, the class $\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{NP}}$ has no linear-size circuits (p.~51, lines 2912--2916).}
``Williams proved that if ETH is false, then $\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{NP}}$
does not have linear-size circuits [Wil13, Theorem 6.1]. Apply this
implication to the deterministic subexponential-time 3-SAT algorithm
supplied by Theorem 1.1.'' \textbf{Supported}: the only input is a
deterministic $2^{o(n)}$ 3-SAT decision, which the verified statement
provides on its answer tape for $k = 3$; the implication is a citation.
The manuscript's own scope sentence applies: the corollary ``does not
assert a superpolynomial circuit lower bound for NP'' (lines 2923--2924).

\paragraph{Corollary C.2, inverting constant-locality maps (p.~52, lines 2974--2977).}
For a Boolean map whose every output bit reads at most $d$ input bits (a ``constant-locality'' map), the number of inputs mapping to a given output, and one such input, in
deterministic time $(n+m)^{O_d(1)}\,2^{O_d(\rho(n))}$ and polynomial
space, for $n \ge 20$. Stated with $\rho(n)$. The proof writes one
clause per forbidden local assignment, a CNF of width at most $d$ on the
original $n$ variables with at most $2^d m$ clauses whose satisfying
assignments are exactly the wanted inputs, and applies Theorem~1.1 with
$k = \max\{2, d\}$ (lines 2979--2988). \textbf{Supported}: the encoding
is on paper; the counter and the exponent are the verified ones. The remark that follows, on Goldreich's candidate one-way functions (functions easy to compute and conjectured hard to invert; p.~53, lines 2994--2999), inherits the same status and the manuscript's own
limit, ``This does not rule out one-wayness against polynomial-time
algorithms'' (line 2999).

\subsection{Appendix D: the contraction route}
\label{sec:beyond-appendix-d}

Appendix~D of the manuscript, ``A sharper bound for exact counting''
(p.~53--64, from line 3001), proves Theorem~1.1 ``by contracting Boolean tensor networks. The two running times reflect the number of wires in the networks'' (lines 3002--3003). A tensor network is a way of writing one large sum as a product of small tables; the terms in the rest of this paragraph are the manuscript's, and we do not define them further. Theorem~D.1 (p.~53, lines
3017--3028) is the contraction theorem: a bipartite tensor network with
at most $Cs$ edges, maximum degree at most $\lceil s^{9/10}\rceil$ and
entries in $(\mathbb{Z}/2^{P_0}\mathbb{Z})[V]/(V^\ell)$ is contracted in
$(s+L)^{O(1)}\,2^{O(s/\sqrt{\log s})}$ bit operations and polynomial
space. The fixed-density proof (p.~54, lines 3045--3064) encodes a
formula as a network with $O_k(n+m)$ edges and applies Theorem~D.1 with
$s = n + m$. The arbitrary-density proof (p.~57, lines 3222--3246)
combines the sparsification of Section~6 with Lemma~D.2 (p.~54, line
3069) and reaches the exponent $O_k(n\log\log n/\sqrt{\log n})$ at line
3243. Section~D.4 changes the logarithm convention for its own proof,
``log denotes a base-two logarithm and ln denotes a natural logarithm''
(p.~57, lines 3251--3252). All of this is \textbf{paper-only}: no
declaration about tensor networks, contraction, Hermite recovery or
packets exists in the family's 3,977 files, by the identifier search of
Section~\ref{sec:beyond-lean}. We make no statement about Appendix~D's
argument; we did not read it for correctness.

\subsection{The hypotheses of \S1.1.1 and the fine-grained remarks of \S1.1.3}
\label{sec:beyond-hypotheses}

\paragraph{\S1.1.1 (p.~4, lines 187--191).}
``Theorem 1.1 refutes ETH, SETH, Gap-ETH, and NSETH. Indeed, testing
whether the exact count is positive or zero decides both satisfiability
and unsatisfiability in deterministic $2^{o(n)}(n+m)^{O_k(1)}$ time for
every fixed $k$; deciding satisfiability also solves the Gap-ETH promise
problem. The deterministic algorithm also refutes the bounded-error
randomized versions of ETH [\ldots] and SETH [\ldots], and of Gap-ETH.''
And \S1.1.2 (p.~5, lines 211--212): ``Our algorithm (Theorem 1.1) solves
\#3-SAT deterministically in $2^{o(n)}\,\mathrm{poly}(n,m)$ time and
therefore refutes \#ETH.'' These are the four named hypotheses about how
hard satisfiability is, their randomised forms, and the counting
hypothesis; Section~\ref{sec:consequences} states each one in words.
\textbf{Supported}, and by the shortest argument in the manuscript. The
verified statement is a deterministic machine. Its answer tape says
whether the count is positive, and its count tape carries the count,
within a subexponential bound, for each fixed $k \ge 2$. Each hypothesis
is the denial of that. The hypotheses themselves are not defined in the
library. The word ``SETH'' occurs nowhere in the collection's Lean. The
word ``ETH'' occurs in this family only in comments (six lines of five
files under \clonefile{SqrtLogSpace/}, \clonefile{Final.lean} line 6
among them) and in the name of the theorem \path{exact_count_eth}. Outside
the family it occurs only in two files of a different family,
\clonefile{lean/OAI/Combinatorics/ParityLifts/}. Those two files take an ETH-like
proposition as a hypothesis, and they are not among the files the proof depends
on, its closure (Section~\ref{sec:proof}; counted by
\repofile{paper3/scripts/beyond_claims.py}).
Section~\ref{sec:consequences} says exactly how the verified statement
negates each one. The two sentences the manuscript adds at lines
191--194, on unconditional lower bounds for restricted proof systems and
on Williams's 2016 MA-SETH protocol, are context, not claims about the
result.

\paragraph{\S1.1.3 (p.~5, lines 213--243).}
After a list of lower bounds proved from ETH or SETH (lines 213--225),
the manuscript writes: ``Our refutation of ETH and SETH removes one basis
for the hardness claims above. Some of the same lower bounds follow from
weaker hypotheses that may still hold'' (lines 226--227). It names two such hypotheses. One is the Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture, a hypothesis about a different problem (lines 229--232). The other is the hypothesis that
unrestricted CNF-SAT admits no $2^{(1-\delta)n}\,\mathrm{poly}(n,m)$
algorithm, and here the manuscript adds: ``Theorem 1.1 gives a running-time guarantee for each
fixed clause width $k$, while this hypothesis allows the width to grow
with $n$'' (lines 235--236). These are \textbf{paper-only} remarks in
the sense that nothing about any other hypothesis or any reduction is in
the library; they are also the manuscript's own statement of what the
result does not do, and Section~\ref{sec:consequences} rests on them.
The one positive transfer, Set Splitting with sets of size at most $k$
by one variable per element and two clauses per set (lines 237--243), is
\textbf{supported} in the same sense as Corollary~1.3: an encoding on
paper into the verified counter, inheriting $\rhon$. The caveat at
p.~2, lines 95--99, that $2^{o(n)}$ bounds ``do not transfer under
general polynomial-time reductions,'' is not a claim but a limit the
manuscript places on its own result; it is quoted again in
Section~\ref{sec:consequences}.

\subsection{What this does not claim}
\label{sec:beyond-not}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No judgement on the paper-only proofs.} Appendix~D,
  Appendix~C.1 and the two sharper bounds of Theorem~1.1 were located
  and quoted, not read for correctness. ``Paper-only'' means not in the
  Lean library at commit \texttt{4eaee875}; it does not mean wrong, and
  nothing here suggests it.
\item \textbf{No judgement on the supported arguments either.} The
  reductions behind Corollaries~1.2, 1.3, C.1, C.2 and the Set Splitting
  transfer are standard in kind and short, and we checked only which
  property of the counter they use. Whether each is correct as written
  is the manuscript's, not ours.
\item \textbf{Nothing about what the verified exponent would yield
  through Corollary~1.4's argument.} A reader can rerun Appendix~C.1
  with $a(t)$ replaced by the verified rate; we did not, because it is
  a question about a paper-only proof.
\item \textbf{No constants.} Every constant in this section is as
  unknown as the comparator's $C, A, S, d, e$; the comparisons of
  Table~\ref{tab:beyond-exponents} are at constant $1$ and say nothing
  about any machine's running time.
\item \textbf{Nothing beyond the collection.} Whether the sharper bounds
  have since been formalised elsewhere, or the manuscript revised, is
  not examined; what is read is the commit and the dated manuscript.
\end{itemize}
