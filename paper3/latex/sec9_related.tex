\subsection{What the collection says about itself}
\label{sec:related-collection}

The collection keeps a dated log, \clonefile{history.md} (56 lines). Under
the heading ``October 26, 2026'' (line 11), the entry for this family is
one bullet, which we quote in full (line 18):
\begin{quote}\small
``\emph{The Exponential Time Hypothesis is False.} The paper gives a
deterministic algorithm that exactly counts satisfying assignments of
fixed-width CNF formulas in subexponential time and polynomial space. The
case of 3-SAT refutes the Exponential Time Hypothesis. An independent
working group of computer scientists led by Russell Impagliazzo,
Ramamohan Paturi, and Ryan Williams will examine and explain the proof.
At the group’s request, we are making the materials public immediately so
others can examine them as well.''
\end{quote}
Three things in this sentence matter for us. First, the group it names is
led by two of the three authors of the papers that stated the hypotheses
(Section~\ref{sec:related-hypotheses}). Second, the verb is ``will'': the
log announces an examination to come. Nothing from that group is in the
clone, and we fetched nothing from it, so this paper has no finding of
theirs to cite, agree with or disagree with. Whatever that group
publishes about the proof will carry more weight on the proof than
Section~\ref{sec:proof} does, which only inspected and replayed. Third,
the log says ``the paper gives \ldots{} subexponential time'' with no
exponent; the exponent is in the scope note.

That scope note is \clonefile{lean/docs/378.md}, seventeen lines, quoted
in full in Section~\ref{sec:beyond}. It is the collection's own statement
of the gap between what Lean proves (the exponent with the squared
$\log\log$ factor) and what the manuscript claims (two sharper
exponents). Its table (line 17) links the result to the one comparator
statement, \clonefile{lean/ComparatorChallenges/SubexponentialSAT.lean}.
So the collection itself records that the verified exponent is the weaker
one. What it does not record is what the weaker exponent means at any
size: that $\rhon$, the verified exponent, is larger than $n$ for every
$n$ between $50$ and $e^{5504}$, so that the verified bound with constant
one sits above $2^n$ at every size anyone will ever run
(Section~\ref{sec:statement}). That is the title's observation, and the
note does not make it.

The third document is \clonefile{lean/ComparatorChallenges/README.md},
ten lines. It says how the collection's checks are run: ``Install
\texttt{comparator}, \texttt{landrun}, and \texttt{lean4export}, and make
them available on \texttt{PATH}.'' (line 3), then from the \texttt{lean/}
directory \texttt{lake update}, \texttt{lake exe cache get}, and
\texttt{lake env comparator} on a family's JSON (lines 7--9; the example
is another family's). The JSON for this family has six fields
(Section~\ref{sec:related-comparator} reads them).

\subsection{The two papers that stated the hypotheses}
\label{sec:related-hypotheses}

ETH, the Exponential Time Hypothesis, says that 3-SAT (deciding whether a
formula whose clauses have three literals each can be satisfied) has no
algorithm whose running time grows slower than every exponential in the
number of variables. SETH, the Strong form, says that as the clause width
grows, the best possible base of the exponential tends to $2$, the base of
trying every assignment. Both are from \citet{ImpagliazzoPaturi2001}; the
standard setting of SETH, and the lemma that lets one move between the
number of variables and the number of clauses, are from
\citet{ImpagliazzoPaturiZane2001}. The manuscript cites both as
\texttt{[IP01]} and \texttt{[IPZ01]} (extract page 66, lines 3733--3736)
and states each hypothesis in its own words in its \S1.1.1, which
Section~\ref{sec:consequences} quotes with page and line. We do not hold
either paper: \repofile{literature/} has no file for them, and
\repofile{literature/MISSING.md} now records both with their DOIs. Every
statement of a hypothesis in this paper is therefore the manuscript's
statement of it, not the original's, and we quote nothing from the two
originals. The manuscript's own account of what motivated SETH is one
sentence we can locate (page 4, lines 170--171): the improvements of the
known algorithms ``over exhaustive search diminish as $k$ grows [IP01,
Open Problems, item 2]''. Exhaustive search is the ``trying all $2^n$
assignments'' of the title.

\subsection{A human audit of an earlier batch of results}
\label{sec:related-audit}

\citet{SienickiSienicki2026} is a published human audit of OpenAI's
AI-generated proofs. We fetched its arXiv abstract page and API record
(v1 of 3 August 2026, v2 of 25 August, v3 of 9 September 2026; 13 pages;
primary class cs.AI). It is not about this family. Its abstract says
what it covers: ``We assess 18 chapter-specific reviews of the ten
mathematical results announced by OpenAI on 1 August 2026, alongside
review standards, Lean formalizations, subsequent research, and
mathematical references.'' The family this paper reads is dated 17
October 2026, revised 25 October, and entered the collection on 26
October (Section~\ref{sec:related-collection}); it is not one of the ten,
and the abstract mentions neither it nor ETH (the script checks). So we
cite the audit for its method of review and for nothing about the proof.

Four sentences of the abstract bear on how this paper was written. On
restraint: ``The article audits this review record without claiming a
complete reconstruction of all ten proofs.'' On the finding: ``No
confirmed substantive mathematical error in a principal result remains in
the examined assessments, although review depth varies and some
dependencies remain partly checked.'' On a hazard of reading proofs
through text extraction: ``In Chapter 6, an apparent polarity error was
withdrawn after an overbar lost during PDF extraction was recovered from
the typeset source.'' And on what confidence should rest on: ``We argue
that confidence should combine formal checking, human reconstruction,
independent mathematical use, and a public record supporting correction
of both proofs and reviews.''

The third of these is a warning we took. This paper reads the manuscript
through a \texttt{pdftotext} extract, the same kind of text in which that
overbar was lost. That is why every quotation from the manuscript carries
the page of the PDF beside the line of the extract, why the Lean is read
from its source files and never from a rendering, and why no claim in
this paper rests on a formula read from the extract alone: the formulas
that matter are the Lean's, re-typed in Section~\ref{sec:statement} from
the \texttt{.lean} file and re-checked by the kernel in
Section~\ref{sec:lean}. Against the fourth sentence, this paper is one of
the four things it lists, formal checking, replayed by us and by a
second kernel, together with a reading of what the checked statement
says. It is not a human reconstruction of the proof: Section~\ref{sec:proof}
inspected the proof's files and did not re-derive the argument. We
fetched the abstract only, and quote nothing from the paper's body.

\subsection{The comparator}
\label{sec:related-comparator}

The comparator is the program the collection used to check that the
solution file proves the challenge statement
\citep{LeanComparator}. We fetched its README from the
\texttt{master} branch of \texttt{leanprover/comparator} on 2026-11-05
(latest commit \texttt{865edc14} of 2026-10-14, ``doc: migration
notice''; the repository is archived and under the Apache-2.0 licence).
Its first lines say the tool has moved: ``This project has been merged
into core as of Lean release v4.35.0 in the form of \texttt{lake
comparator} and the accompanying \texttt{lake check}'' (line 3). The
collection pins Lean v4.34.1 (Section~\ref{sec:proof}), one release
earlier, so the stand-alone tool is the one its README names. The README
opens: ``Comparator is a trustworthy judge for Lean proofs.'' (line 8).

What it checks is stated as three guarantees for the theorems a JSON
configuration names (lines 52--55):
\begin{quote}\small
``All theorems in \texttt{Solution} that are listed in
\texttt{theorem\_names} are guaranteed to:\\
1. Prove the same statement as provided in \texttt{Challenge}\\
2. Use no more axioms than listed in \texttt{permitted\_axioms}\\
3. Be accepted by the Lean kernel''
\end{quote}
In plain words: the solution proves exactly the sentence
the challenge posed, uses only the agreed axioms (the three standard ones
in the example configuration, line 27), and passes the kernel, the small
core of Lean that checks proofs. How it checks them is in its
``Internals'' (lines 184--200), six steps: build the challenge inside a
sandbox; export its declarations with \texttt{lean4export}; do the same
for the solution; ``Verify that all declarations used in the statement of
all relevant theorems in \texttt{Challenge} are the same as in the
\texttt{Solution} environment'' (lines 191--192); ``Verify that the body
of all relevant theorems in the \texttt{Solution} environment only uses
axioms listed in \texttt{permitted\_axioms}'' (lines 195--196); and
``Replay the \texttt{Solution} environment into the Lean kernel'' (line
197). The first guarantee is therefore a match of the statement's
declarations as exported, not a textual comparison of the two files.
That is the check Section~\ref{sec:proof} says we did not reproduce: our
statement match is textual (Table~\ref{tab:proof-trust}, row (iv)).

The guarantees hold under six stated assumptions (lines 34--45). The one
that concerns the kernel is the fifth: ``The Lean kernel is correct (with
\texttt{external\_kernels} this can be reduced to `At least one of the
Lean kernel or the \texttt{external\_kernels} is correct')'' (lines
43--44). The sandbox is \texttt{landrun} (line 10), a tool that confines
the build so that a malicious solution file cannot tamper with the
checker; the README notes that its trusted base ``naturally includes the
operating system and hardware it is running on, plus its sandboxing
mechanism'' (line 58). External kernels are optional: ``Comparator can
additionally check solutions with external kernels'' (line 70), and ``For
backwards compatibility reasons users may instead set
\texttt{enable\_nanoda: true} to obtain a config that calls
\texttt{nanoda\_bin}'' (lines 86--87). The tool ``was originally
developed by Lean FRO, with feedback from the AIMO team, in support of
the AIMO series of competitions'' (lines 207--208).

For this family the configuration has six fields
(\clonefile{lean/ComparatorChallenges/SubexponentialSAT.json}): the
challenge module; the solution module,
\path{OAI.Algorithms.SubexponentialSAT.Main}; one theorem name,
\path{SqrtLogSpace.exact_count_and_sat}; an empty
\texttt{definition\_names}; the three standard axioms as
\texttt{permitted\_axioms}; and \texttt{enable\_nanoda: false}. There is
no \texttt{external\_kernels} field. So the check the collection
configured for this family is the Lean kernel alone, under the fifth
assumption in its unweakened form. Section~\ref{sec:replay} is the
weakened form, supplied by us: the same theorem's dependencies through a
second kernel. Our \texttt{bwrap} sandbox there played the role
\texttt{landrun} plays here, for the same reason, and we did not run the
comparator itself.

\subsection{nanoda}
\label{sec:related-nanoda}

nanoda is the second checker \citep{Nanoda}. Its README, fetched from
the \texttt{master} branch of \texttt{ammkrn/nanoda\_lib} on 2026-11-05,
says what it is in one sentence (line 3): ``This is an external type
checker for the Lean 4 programming language and theorem prover.'' The
repository's description line is ``Library implementing type
inference/checking functionality based on the Lean theorem prover''; the
licence is Apache-2.0. It reads the file \texttt{lean4export} writes:
``Export files can be created with lean4export'' (line 13), and ``Given
that lean4export already uses json as its export format, adding a json
parser as a dependency doesn't increase the overall complexity of the
project'' (line 23). Its axiom rule is the comparator's: ``The average
Lean user will want to permit the three semi-official axioms, which are
\texttt{Quot.sound}, \texttt{Classical.choice}, and \texttt{propext}, and
most export files will also rely on \texttt{Lean.trustCompiler}'' (line
33); the export we checked contains the first three and no fourth
(Section~\ref{sec:replay}). Two kernel extensions for arithmetic on
literals are off by default and ``enable or disable the Nat and String
kernel extensions'' (line 39); the replay turned both on.

The latest commit of the repository at the time of the fetch is
\texttt{8de7e0c4} of 2026-10-29, and that is the commit the replay built
and ran (version 0.4.31; the script checks the replay's log against the
fetched record). The checker is written in Rust and shares no code with
the Lean kernel, which is what makes it a second opinion. Its author's
book on external type checkers, linked from line 3 of the README, was not
fetched.

\subsection{Where we looked for an earlier statement of the title's observation}
\label{sec:related-search}

The title says the verified guarantee is weaker than trying all $2^n$
assignments at every practical size. We looked for an earlier statement
of that observation in the texts this paper rests on, and in no others:
the collection's \clonefile{history.md}, its scope note
\clonefile{lean/docs/378.md}, the abstract of
\citet{SienickiSienicki2026}, the comparator README, the nanoda README,
and the manuscript extract. The script searches each for the words such
a statement would be likely to use (``practical'', ``brute'', ``trying
all'', ``exhaustive'', ``$2^n$'', ``constants are'', ``concrete''). Outside
the manuscript, none occurs. In the manuscript, ``practical'' occurs once
(page 4, line 153), in a sentence about the practical value of SAT
solvers on industrial formulas, a different sense; ``exhaustive'' occurs
eleven times, as the motivation of SETH quoted above (line 170) and as the
proof's own fallback for small inputs (lines 2480 and 2487, where the
algorithm uses ``exhaustive search'' below the cutoff
$n \ge 20$). The nearest earlier statements are the manuscript's own
sentence that Lean verifies ``the weaker exponent'' (page 2, lines
63--67; Section~\ref{sec:beyond}) and the scope note: both say the
verified exponent is the weaker one, and neither says at what sizes the
verified bound exceeds $2^n$, which is the title's point. Within these
texts we found no prior statement of it. The search is bounded by that
list: we did not search the literature at large, the working group's
output, or the press.

\subsection{What this does not claim}
\label{sec:related-not}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Nothing about the working group's findings.} The
  collection's log says the group ``will examine and explain the
  proof''; we hold nothing it has written, and we neither anticipate
  nor contradict it.
\item \textbf{The human audit does not cover this family.} It is cited
  for its method and its stated lesson about PDF extraction; its body
  was not fetched, and no sentence of it beyond the abstract is quoted.
\item \textbf{The two hypothesis papers were not read.} They are cited
  as the manuscript cites them, and the hypotheses are stated as the
  manuscript states them (Section~\ref{sec:consequences}).
\item \textbf{We did not run the comparator.} What we reproduced is
  stated in Section~\ref{sec:proof}'s Table~\ref{tab:proof-trust};
  the comparator's statement match by exported declarations is not among
  it.
\item \textbf{No claim that landrun, lean4export or nanoda is correct.}
  Each is quoted for what its README says it does; the replay's
  evidence is that two independent checkers agreed on this export, and
  the trust left in \texttt{lean4export} is stated in
  Section~\ref{sec:replay}.
\item \textbf{``No prior statement'' is bounded by the list above.} Six
  texts, read with a script; not a literature search.
\end{itemize}
