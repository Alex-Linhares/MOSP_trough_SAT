\section{For the practitioner}
\label{sec:practitioner}

The title compares two guarantees: the verified one,
$C(L+1)^d\,2^{A\rhon}$ with $C, A, d$ existential (comparator file lines
136--139 and 210--211, Section~\ref{sec:statement}), and the trivial one,
$2^n \cdot \mathrm{poly}(n+m)$ for trying every assignment. Neither is what
a practitioner runs. What runs on a machine, for the decision bit, is a
conflict-driven clause-learning (CDCL) solver, and for the count an exact
or approximate model counter; and no implementation of the verified
machine exists to run beside them. This section measures the tools a
practitioner has, on random 3-CNF, and draws the two guarantees next to
the measurements as shapes, so that the reader can see where each sits.
The point is small and the section says it once: the verified guarantee
is not the binding one on any machine, because it is above $2^n$ for
every $n \ge 50$ (Section~\ref{sec:statement}) while the solvers are many
orders of magnitude below $2^n$ at every size they were run at.

\subsection{Why the comparison is between guarantees and measurements}
\label{sec:practitioner-why}

The verified statement is existential in the machine (comparator line
195 opens with \texttt{$\exists$ (machine : \ldots)}, Section~\ref{sec:statement}) and
the solution's
3,977 files contain no \texttt{\#eval}, no \texttt{def main} and no
\texttt{IO} action (checked read-only by the script, which reads every
file of the family with \texttt{open()} and counts those three patterns:
0, 0, 0). The library proves that a machine with the stated bound
exists and exhibits one as a Lean term inside a development with 1,048
\texttt{noncomputable} declarations and no executable interface
(Section~\ref{sec:proof}); nothing in the clone runs it on an input, and
we found no implementation elsewhere. So the verified algorithm's actual
running time on any formula is unknown, in both directions: nothing says
it is slow, and nothing says it is fast. What is known is its
\emph{guarantee}, and a guarantee is compared with other guarantees. The
measurements below are of other programs entirely; they are in the
section because the question a practitioner asks is not ``which
guarantee is smaller'' but ``what would I run'', and the answer to that
has not changed.

\subsection{What was run}
\label{sec:practitioner-setup}

\paragraph{Instances.} Random 3-CNF on $n$ variables with
$m = \lfloor 4.26\,n \rceil$ clauses, each clause three distinct variables
with uniform signs, generated by the script from $(n, \mathrm{seed})$;
twenty seeds per size; $n = 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 80, 100, 150, 200, 250,
300, 400, 500$. The ratio $4.26$ is the empirical satisfiability
threshold of random 3-SAT \citep{Selman1996Hard}, where decision is
hardest for a given $n$ among random formulas (the cavity method puts the
threshold at $4.2667$, \citealp{Mertens2006Threshold}); the sizes are
chosen so that the smallest ones are where the title's crossing is
($n = 50$) and the largest are where a CDCL solver on random formulas
begins to take seconds. These are random instances only; nothing is
claimed about structured formulas.

\paragraph{Tools.} CaDiCaL 1.9.5 \citep{Biere2024CaDiCaL} through
python-sat 1.9.dev15 \citep{Ignatiev2018PySAT}, one \texttt{solve()} call,
for the decision bit $[0 < \mathtt{count}\ F]$; Ganak 2.8.0
\citep{Sharma2019Ganak}, through the pyganak binding (which runs its Arjun
preprocessing and then the counter), for the exact count; ApproxMC 4.4.0
\citep{Chakraborty2013ApproxMC, Soos2019BIRD} at its defaults
$\varepsilon = 0.8$, $\delta = 0.2$ with the projection set equal to all
variables, for an approximate count; and, at $n \le 30$ only, exact
counting by enumeration over CaDiCaL with a blocking clause per model,
which is not a counter at any larger size (it is linear in the count,
and the count is exponential in $n$) and is here as the independent check
of the two counters where it can reach. Ganak and ApproxMC were installed
from PyPI for this section; no \texttt{sharpSAT}, \texttt{d4} or other
counter binary is on the host.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Each call runs in its own interpreter under
\texttt{nice -n 19}, at most eight at a time on the thirty-two cores of
an AMD Ryzen 9 9950X (Linux 7.0.0, Python 3.12.13; the machine was shared
with other work, load average at the start in
\repofile{tables/practitioner_meta.json}), with a wall-clock budget of
60~s per call; the time recorded is \texttt{perf\_counter} around the
construction of the solver object and the one call, inside the child,
with the interpreter's start-up and the import outside it. A call over
budget is killed and recorded as censored; a size at which a tool finishes
fewer than ten of its twenty instances ends that tool's escalation. The
whole run took about twenty minutes (1,172~s) minutes.

\paragraph{The checks.} On every instance where both finished, Ganak's
count equals the enumeration's (40 instances at $n \le 30$) and
CaDiCaL's bit equals $[\mathrm{count} > 0]$; ApproxMC's estimate is
within its factor $1 + \varepsilon = 1.8$ of Ganak's on 178 of the
206 instances where both finished (it promises that with
probability $1 - \delta = 0.8$ per instance). The script exits non-zero
if any of these fails.

\paragraph{What the solvers did.} CaDiCaL's median is under a
millisecond up to $n = 80$, 2.5~ms at 100, 58~ms at 200, 1.5~s at 250
and 3.7~s at 300 (maximum 20.5~s); at 400 it finishes 10 of 20 within
the budget and at 500 8 of 20, every finished one satisfiable, which is
the known shape of CDCL on random formulas at the threshold (the
unsatisfiable half is the expensive half). Ganak finishes all 20 up to
$n = 250$ (median 17.6~s there, maximum 45.7~s), 10 of 20 at 300 and
none at 400; its median is flat at 8.3~s and 6.2~s at $n = 100$ and
150 and lower, 2.4~s, at 200, while its maximum rises throughout ---
the plateau looks like a fixed budget inside the binding's
preprocessing rather than counting difficulty, and we report it as
measured and tuned nothing (\texttt{ETH\_AMBIGUOUS.MD}, 06d). Where the
count is positive it is small for the size: at $n = 200$ the fifteen
satisfiable instances have between 372 and $2.5 \times 10^{12}$ models
out of $2^{200}$. ApproxMC finishes all 20 up to $n = 200$ (median
0.51~s there), 18 at 250, 11 at 300 and none at 400. The sizes at which
the three tools leave the budget --- 300 for the counters, 400 for the
decision --- are where this measurement ends; nothing is claimed beyond
them.

\subsection{The two guarantees beside them}
\label{sec:practitioner-guarantees}

The guarantees are drawn as shapes. Both are taken at constant 1 --- the
polynomial factors, $C$, $A$ and $d$ are all set aside --- and both are
multiplied by the same factor $t_{20}/2^{20}$, where $t_{20}$ is
CaDiCaL's median at $n = 20$. The factor is one number for both curves,
so their ratio is the ratio of the guarantees themselves,
$2^{\rhon - n}$: below 1 for $n < 50$, equal to 1 at $n = 50$ up to the
rounding of $\rho(50) = 50.13$, and above 1 from there (it is $2.8 \times 10^{3}$ at
$n = 100$ and $4.0 \times 10^{51}$ at $n = 500$). A reader who prefers each curve
scaled to the $n = 20$ point on its own would multiply $2^{\rhon}$ by a
further $2^{20 - \rho(20)} = 2^{2.79}$ and see the two curves touch at
$n = 20$ and the verified one above the trivial one from there on; the
common factor is the honest choice because it keeps the crossing where
the statement puts it (\texttt{ETH\_AMBIGUOUS.MD}, 06a).

In that scale the brute-force guarantee reaches one second near
$n = 33$ and the verified guarantee near $n = 35$; at $n = 60$,
the largest size of the title's practical range that this section's
smallest tools were run at, the measured CaDiCaL median is $3 \times 10^{-4}$ s against
$2^n$ at $1.4 \times 10^{8}$ s and $2^{\rhon}$ at $5.4 \times 10^{8}$ s, 11.6 and 12.2 decades
respectively; at $n = 500$ the decision median is above the budget (8 of 20 finished within 60~s), against
$10^{140.6}$ s and $10^{192.2}$ s. Nothing in those decades is about the
verified machine: the gap is between what a CDCL solver does on random
formulas and what any $2^{\Theta(n)}$ guarantee says, and it would be
the same gap for the brute-force guarantee alone. The one fact this
section adds to the title is the order of the three objects: at every
size in the table the measurement is far below $2^n$, and $2^{\rhon}$ is
above $2^n$ from $n = 50$ on, so the verified guarantee is the largest
of the three at every practical size and the measurement the smallest.

One remark on what the verified machine's \emph{running time} would have
to be, were it ever run. Nothing in this section bears on it: the
constants $C, A, d$ are unknown (Section~\ref{sec:statement}), the
machine is a Lean term, and an existential statement is not an
implementation. The only values on record are the proof's own, $A \ge
257$ and $C \ge 28{,}701$ (Sections~\ref{sec:statement} and
\ref{sec:proof}); a guarantee of $2^{257\,\rhon}$ steps is a guarantee
and not a measurement, and a different proof of the same statement could
carry any positive constants.

\subsection{What this does not claim}
\label{sec:practitioner-not}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Timings on one machine, one day, named versions.} The
  seconds are from one AMD Ryzen 9 9950X shared with other work, under
  \texttt{nice -n 19} with eight calls in flight, through Python
  bindings; they are reproducible in shape and not in value, and the
  versions are those named in Section~\ref{sec:practitioner-setup} and
  in \repofile{tables/practitioner_meta.json}.
\item \textbf{Random instances only.} Random 3-CNF at the threshold ratio
  is one distribution; structured formulas can be harder for CDCL at
  far smaller $n$ and easier for counters. Nothing is claimed about any
  other distribution, and nothing about worst-case behaviour.
\item \textbf{Nothing about the verified machine's running time.} It is
  unknown, in both directions; no implementation exists to run; the
  guarantees drawn are at constant 1 and the constants are existential.
\item \textbf{Nothing about $k > 3$.} The statement is for every fixed
  $k$; the measurement is at $k = 3$.
\item \textbf{The counters are reported, not tuned.} Ganak ran through
  its binding at defaults, preprocessing included; ApproxMC at its
  default tolerance; a different configuration gives different seconds.
  The check against enumeration is at $n \le 30$ only.
\item \textbf{No statement about which tool a practitioner should use.}
  The section shows that the verified guarantee is not binding; it does
  not rank solvers.
\end{itemize}
