\section{For the practitioner}
\label{sec:practitioner}

The title compares two guarantees. One is the verified bound,
$C(L+1)^d\,2^{A\rhon}$, with $C$, $A$ and $d$ never computed (comparator
file lines 136--139 and 210--211, Section~\ref{sec:statement}). The
other is the trivial bound for trying every assignment, $2^n$ times a
polynomial. Neither is what a practitioner runs. What runs on a real
machine, when the question is whether a formula can be satisfied, is a
\emph{SAT solver}; the standard kind is called CDCL, for
conflict-driven clause learning, a search that learns from each dead
end it reaches. When the question is how many satisfying assignments
there are, what runs is a \emph{model counter}, exact or approximate.
No implementation of the verified machine exists to run beside them.
This section measures the tools a practitioner has, on random formulas,
and draws the two guarantees next to the measurements as shapes, so
that the reader can see where each sits. The point is small and the section says it once. The verified guarantee is not the binding one on any machine, because it is above $2^n$ for every $n \ge 50$
(Section~\ref{sec:statement}). The solvers, meanwhile, are many orders of
magnitude below $2^n$ at every size they were run at.

\subsection{Why the comparison is between guarantees and measurements}
\label{sec:practitioner-why}

The verified statement says a machine \emph{exists} (comparator line 195
opens with the existence quantifier, Section~\ref{sec:statement}). The
solution's 3,977 files contain no command to run anything: no
\texttt{\#eval}, no \texttt{def main} and no input-output (\texttt{IO})
action. The
script of this section checks that, reading every file of the family
with \texttt{open()} and counting those three patterns, each as a whole
word at the start of a line or before a type (so \texttt{def mainProgram}
is not \texttt{def main}): 0, 0, 0. The
library proves that a machine with the stated bound exists, and exhibits
one as a mathematical term. That term sits inside a development with 1,048
\texttt{noncomputable} declarations and no way to execute anything
(Section~\ref{sec:proof}). Nothing in the clone runs it on an input, and
we found no implementation elsewhere. So the verified algorithm's actual
running time on any formula is unknown, in both directions: nothing says
it is slow, and nothing says it is fast. What is known is its
\emph{guarantee}, and a guarantee is compared with other guarantees. The
measurements below are of other programs entirely. They are in the
section because the question a practitioner asks is not ``which
guarantee is smaller'' but ``what would I run'', and the answer to that
has not changed.

\subsection{What was run}
\label{sec:practitioner-setup}

\paragraph{Instances.} Random 3-CNF on $n$ variables with $m = \lfloor
4.26\,n \rceil$ clauses ($4.26\,n$ rounded to the nearest whole number), each clause three distinct variables with
random signs, generated by the script from $(n, \mathrm{seed})$; twenty
seeds per size; $n = 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 80, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300,
400, 500$. The ratio $4.26$ clauses per variable is where random 3-SAT
switches from mostly satisfiable to mostly unsatisfiable
\citep{Selman1996Hard}; random formulas are hardest to decide there
(a method from statistical physics puts the threshold at $4.2667$, \citealp{Mertens2006Threshold}). The sizes are chosen so that the
smallest ones are where the title's crossing is ($n = 50$) and the
largest are where a CDCL solver on random formulas begins to take
seconds. These are random instances only; nothing is claimed about
formulas with structure.

\paragraph{Tools.} CaDiCaL 1.9.5 \citep{Biere2024CaDiCaL}, a CDCL solver,
gives the yes-or-no answer, through python-sat 1.9.dev15
\citep{Ignatiev2018PySAT} in one \texttt{solve()} call. Ganak 2.8.0
\citep{Sharma2019Ganak}, an exact model counter, gives the exact count,
through the pyganak binding (which runs a simplification pass called
Arjun and then the counter). ApproxMC 4.4.0 \citep{Chakraborty2013ApproxMC,
Soos2019BIRD}, an approximate counter, runs at its defaults $\varepsilon =
0.8$, $\delta = 0.2$ (an answer within a factor $1.8$ of the truth with
probability $0.8$), with the count taken over all variables (its
``projection set''). And at $n \le 30$ only, the exact count is also
found by listing every solution with CaDiCaL, one at a time. That is not
a counter at any larger size, since its time grows with the count and the
count grows exponentially in $n$; it is here as the independent check of
the two counters where it can reach. Ganak and ApproxMC were installed from PyPI for this section; no
\texttt{sharpSAT}, \texttt{d4} or other counter binary is on the host.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Each call runs in its own interpreter under
\texttt{nice -n 19}, at most eight at a time, with a wall-clock budget of
60~s per call. The machine is an AMD Ryzen 9 9950X with thirty-two cores
(Linux 7.0.0, Python 3.12.13). It was
shared with other work; the load average at the start is in
\repofile{tables/practitioner_meta.json}. The time recorded is the wall clock (\texttt{perf\_counter}) around the
construction of the solver object and the one call, inside the child
process, with the interpreter's start-up and the import outside it. A
call over budget is killed and recorded as \emph{censored}, that is, as
``more than 60~s''. A size at which a tool finishes fewer than ten of
its twenty instances ends that tool's climb to larger sizes. The whole
run took about twenty minutes (1,172~s).

\paragraph{The checks.} On every instance where both finished, Ganak's
count equals the count by listing (40 instances at $n \le 30$) and
CaDiCaL's answer equals ``count $> 0$''. ApproxMC's estimate is within
its factor $1 + \varepsilon = 1.8$ of Ganak's on 178 of the 206
instances where both finished (it promises that with probability
$1 - \delta = 0.8$ per instance). The script exits non-zero if any of
these fails.

\paragraph{What the solvers did.} CaDiCaL's median is under a
millisecond up to $n = 80$, 2.5~ms at 100, 58~ms at 200, 1.5~s at 250
and 3.7~s at 300 (maximum 20.5~s). At 400 it finishes 10 of 20 within
the budget and at 500 8 of 20, every finished one satisfiable. That is
the known shape of CDCL on random formulas at the threshold: the
unsatisfiable half is the expensive half. Ganak finishes all 20 up to
$n = 250$ (median 17.6~s there, maximum 45.7~s), 10 of 20 at 300 and
none at 400; its median is flat at 8.3~s and 6.2~s at $n = 100$ and
150 and lower, 2.4~s, at 200, while its maximum rises throughout. The
flat stretch looks like a fixed time budget inside the binding's
preprocessing rather than counting difficulty; we report it as measured
and tuned nothing (\texttt{ETH\_AMBIGUOUS.MD}, 06d). Where the count is
positive it is small for the size: at $n = 200$ the fifteen satisfiable
instances have between 372 and $2.5 \times 10^{12}$ solutions out of
$2^{200}$ assignments. ApproxMC finishes all 20 up to $n = 200$ (median
0.51~s there), 18 at 250, 11 at 300 and none at 400. The sizes at which
the three tools leave the budget, 300 for the counters and 400 for the
decision, are where this measurement ends; nothing is claimed beyond
them.

\subsection{The two guarantees beside them}
\label{sec:practitioner-guarantees}

The guarantees are drawn as shapes. Both are taken with constant $1$
(the polynomial factors, $C$, $A$ and $d$ are all set aside), and both
are multiplied by the same factor, chosen so that $2^n$ passes through
CaDiCaL's median at $n = 20$. Because the factor is one number for both
curves, their ratio is the ratio of the guarantees themselves,
$2^{\rhon - n}$: below $1$ for $n < 50$, equal to $1$ at $n = 50$ up to
rounding, and above $1$ from there (it is $2.8 \times 10^{3}$ at
$n = 100$ and $4.0 \times 10^{51}$ at $n = 500$). Scaling each curve to
the $n = 20$ point on its own would instead make the two touch at
$n = 20$ and put the verified one above from there on. The common
factor is the honest choice, because it keeps the crossing where the
statement puts it (Appendix~\ref{app:tech-scaling};
\texttt{ETH\_AMBIGUOUS.MD}, 06a).

In that scale the brute-force guarantee reaches one second near
$n = 33$ and the verified guarantee near $n = 35$. At $n = 60$, the
measured CaDiCaL median is $3 \times 10^{-4}$~s, against $1.4 \times
10^{8}$~s for $2^n$ and $5.4 \times 10^{8}$~s for $2^{\rhon}$: the
measurement is below the two guarantees by factors of $10^{11.6}$ and
$10^{12.2}$. At $n = 500$ the decision median is above the budget (8 of
20 finished within 60~s), against $10^{140.6}$~s and $10^{192.2}$~s.
Nothing in those enormous factors is about the verified machine: the
gap is between what a CDCL solver does on random formulas and what any
exponential guarantee says, and it would be the same gap for the
brute-force guarantee alone. The one fact this section adds to the
title is the order of the three objects. At every size in the table the
measurement is far below $2^n$, and $2^{\rhon}$ is above $2^n$ from
$n = 50$ on. So the verified guarantee is the largest of the three at
every practical size, and the measurement the smallest.

One remark on what the verified machine's \emph{running time} would
have to be, were it ever run. Nothing in this section bears on it: the
constants $C, A, d$ are unknown (Section~\ref{sec:statement}), the
machine is a mathematical term, and a statement that something exists
is not a program. The only values on record are the proof's own:
$A \ge 257$ in its construction (Section~\ref{sec:statement}), and
$A \ge 258$ with $C \ge 28{,}701$ in the theorem it exhibits
(Section~\ref{sec:proof}). A guarantee of $2^{257\,\rhon}$ steps is a guarantee
and not a measurement. A different proof of the same statement
could carry any positive constants.

\subsection{What this does not claim}
\label{sec:practitioner-not}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Timings on one machine, one day, named versions.} The
  seconds are from one AMD Ryzen 9 9950X shared with other work, under
  \texttt{nice -n 19} with eight calls in flight, through Python
  bindings; they are reproducible in shape and not in value, and the
  versions are those named in Section~\ref{sec:practitioner-setup} and
  in \repofile{tables/practitioner_meta.json}.
\item \textbf{Random instances only.} Random 3-CNF at the threshold
  ratio is one distribution; formulas with structure can be harder for
  CDCL at far smaller $n$ and easier for counters. Nothing is claimed
  about any other distribution, and nothing about worst-case behaviour.
\item \textbf{Nothing about the verified machine's running time.} It is
  unknown, in both directions; no implementation exists to run; the
  guarantees drawn are at constant $1$ and the constants are never
  computed.
\item \textbf{Nothing about $k > 3$.} The statement is for every fixed
  $k$; the measurement is at $k = 3$.
\item \textbf{The counters are reported, not tuned.} Ganak ran through
  its binding at defaults, preprocessing included; ApproxMC at its
  default tolerance; a different configuration gives different seconds.
  The check against listing is at $n \le 30$ only.
\item \textbf{No statement about which tool a practitioner should use.}
  The section shows that the verified guarantee is not the binding one;
  it does not rank solvers.
\end{itemize}
