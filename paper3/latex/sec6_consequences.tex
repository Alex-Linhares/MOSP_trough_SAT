\section{What follows, in general terms}
\label{sec:consequences}

Sections~\ref{sec:statement}--\ref{sec:replay} read the verified
statement, drew the line between it and the manuscript, inspected the
proof and replayed it. This section asks what follows from the verified
statement alone. Two things: which of the standard hardness hypotheses
it refutes, and by what step; and what a lower bound that was derived
from one of those hypotheses has lost. The manuscript answers both
questions itself, in its \S1.1.1--\S1.1.2 (p.~4--5) and \S1.1.3 (p.~5),
and we quote it. What we add is the exact logical form of each
hypothesis, the one line of the comparator statement that denies it, and
the distinction, stated in general terms, between a theorem whose
premise has become false and a statement that has been refuted. Nothing
here names a problem the manuscript's \S1.1.1 and \S1.1.3 do not name,
and nothing here is about any problem's complexity beyond what those two
sections state.

A \emph{hypothesis}, in this section, is a conjecture about how hard
satisfiability is. The Exponential Time Hypothesis, ETH, says that
3-SAT needs time exponential in the number of variables. Its strong
form, SETH, says that as the clause width $k$ grows, no algorithm for
$k$-SAT does much better than trying all $2^n$ assignments. Several
variants exist, and the manuscript lists them. A \emph{lower bound
derived from a hypothesis} is a theorem of the form ``if the hypothesis
holds, then problem $X$ has no fast algorithm''; hundreds of such
theorems exist in the literature.

\subsection{The one property every refutation uses}
\label{sec:consequences-property}

Every refutation below rests on the same parts of the verified theorem
(comparator file lines 194--217, Section~\ref{sec:statement}). For each
fixed width $k \ge 2$ there is one deterministic machine which, given
any formula of width at most $k$ on $n$ variables, halts with the number
of satisfying assignments on its count tape (lines 203--204), the
answer ``is that number positive'' on its answer tape and in its
halting state (lines 205--207), within the bound $C\,(L+1)^d\,2^{A\rhon}$
(lines 210--211). Four features of that property do the work.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{It is deterministic.} The step rule is a function; there
  is no random choice and no guess. A deterministic algorithm is also a
  randomised algorithm, one that happens never to err, and also a
  nondeterministic algorithm, one that happens never to guess. So each
  refutation of a hypothesis about deterministic algorithms is a
  refutation of its randomised and nondeterministic forms by the same
  machine.
\item \textbf{The bound is subexponential in $n$ alone.} A formula of
  width $k$ on $n$ variables has at most about $n^k$ distinct clauses
  ($2{,}604{,}376$ at $n = 125$, $k = 3$), so the input length $L$ is at
  most a polynomial in $n$, and the whole bound is a polynomial in $n$
  times $2^{A\rhon}$. Since $\rhon/n$ goes to zero
  \leanref{rho-little-o}, for every rate $\delta > 0$ there is a size
  beyond which the bound is below $2^{\delta n}$; below that size the
  bound is a constant depending on $k$ and $\delta$. In the standard
  notation, the running time is $O_{k,\delta}(2^{\delta n})$ for every
  $\delta > 0$ (Appendix~\ref{app:tech-hypotheses}). This is a fact
  about the limit, and the hypotheses are statements about the limit.
  It coexists with Section~\ref{sec:statement}'s observation that
  $\rhon > n$ on every $50 \le n < e^{5504}$, which is about where the
  limit begins.
\item \textbf{One machine per $k$, for every $n$.} The machine and the
  constants are chosen after $k$ and before $n$
  (Section~\ref{sec:statement}); there is no $n \ge 20$ condition in the
  comparator statement, though the manuscript's Theorem~1.1 carries one
  (p.~2, lines 56--57). So the verified form is the strong form of the denial, one algorithm for every rate at once: a single algorithm that runs within $2^{\delta n}$ times a
  polynomial for every $\delta$ at once, where the denial of a
  hypothesis of the form ``for every $\delta$ there is an algorithm''
  would have allowed a different algorithm for each $\delta$.
\item \textbf{The machine model is the conservative one.} The
  comparator's machine is a multitape Turing machine that moves one head
  one cell or writes one symbol per step. Every step of such a machine is a bounded amount of work on any of the standard models of computation (random-access machines, other kinds of Turing machine) in which the hypotheses are stated, so the bound transfers to those models with at
  most a constant factor per step, which the polynomial absorbs. The
  input is the manuscript's own encoding, of length polynomial in
  $n + m$; converting from any other reasonable encoding is polynomial
  too.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The hypotheses, one by one}
\label{sec:consequences-hypotheses}

The manuscript's own sentence is (p.~4, lines 187--191):
\begin{quote}\small
``Theorem 1.1 refutes ETH, SETH, Gap-ETH, and NSETH. Indeed, testing
whether the exact count is positive or zero decides both satisfiability
and unsatisfiability in deterministic $2^{o(n)}(n+m)^{O_k(1)}$ time for
every fixed $k$; deciding satisfiability also solves the Gap-ETH promise
problem. The deterministic algorithm also refutes the bounded-error
randomized versions of ETH [DHM$^+$14, Section 1] and SETH [CDL$^+$16,
Definition 2.1], and of Gap-ETH.''
\end{quote}
and, for counting (p.~5, lines 211--212): ``Our algorithm (Theorem 1.1)
solves \#3-SAT deterministically in $2^{o(n)}\,\mathrm{poly}(n,m)$ time
and therefore refutes \#ETH.'' We take each hypothesis in the form the
manuscript states it, with the manuscript's own source for that form,
and say which part of the verified theorem denies it.
Table~\ref{tab:hypotheses} is the summary, and
Appendix~\ref{app:tech-hypotheses} gives each hypothesis and its denial
with the quantifiers written out. In every case the only thing used is
the property of Section~\ref{sec:consequences-property}; nothing is used
from the manuscript's sharper exponents, which are not verified
(Section~\ref{sec:beyond}).

\paragraph{ETH.}
As stated (p.~4, lines 165--166): ``The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH)
asserted that 3-SAT requires $2^{\Omega(n)}$ deterministic time in the
worst case, and hence so does $k$-SAT for every fixed $k \ge 3$ [IP01,
Theorem~1],'' citing \citet{ImpagliazzoPaturi2001}. In words: there is
some fixed rate $\delta > 0$ such that no deterministic algorithm decides
3-SAT within $2^{\delta n}$ times a polynomial, on all inputs. Its
denial: for every rate $\delta > 0$ there is such an algorithm. The
verified statement at $k = 3$ supplies, for every $\delta$, the same
machine, whose answer bit is the decision, within that time (item 2
above). This holds on every input of every size, so it refutes the
hypothesis whether ``requires $2^{\Omega(n)}$ time'' is read as a lower
bound at infinitely many sizes or at all large sizes. In the vocabulary
of \citet{ImpagliazzoPaturi2001}, where $s_k$ is the smallest rate $\delta$ such that $k$-SAT has an algorithm within $2^{\delta n}$ and ETH is the statement that $s_3 > 0$: the verified statement gives $s_k = 0$
for every $k \ge 2$, by one algorithm per $k$.

\paragraph{SETH.}
As stated (p.~4, lines 166--169): ``The Strong Exponential Time
Hypothesis (SETH) made the sharper prediction that $k$-SAT requires
$2^{n(1-o_k(1))}$ time as $k$ grows. Precisely, for every
$\varepsilon \in (0,1)$, there is a fixed $k$ for which no deterministic
algorithm solves $k$-SAT in $2^{(1-\varepsilon)n}(n+m)^{O(1)}$ time,''
with the motivation attributed to \citet{ImpagliazzoPaturi2001}'s open
problems (lines 169--171). In words: however small a saving
$\varepsilon$ one asks for over trying all $2^n$ assignments, some clause
width $k$ makes that saving impossible. Its denial: there is a saving
$\varepsilon$ that every fixed width $k$ allows. The verified statement
gives more: for \emph{every} saving $\varepsilon$ and every $k \ge 2$,
the machine for $k$ runs within $2^{(1-\varepsilon)n}$ times a
polynomial for all large $n$, because $\rhon$ grows more slowly than
$n$; below that size the exponential factor is a constant. The
polynomial's degree depends on $k$, and that is exactly what the
hypothesis's ``for a fixed $k$'' permits. The refutation does not pass
through ETH; it reads the statement at each $k$. It is also standard
that SETH implies ETH, through the Sparsification Lemma of \citet{ImpagliazzoPaturiZane2001}, a tool that trades one formula with many clauses for a few formulas with few clauses (Appendix~\ref{app:tech-hypotheses});
so the refutation of ETH alone would already refute SETH. We note the
implication for the record; the direct reading above is what the paper
rests on.

\paragraph{Gap-ETH, and its randomised form.}
As stated (p.~4, lines 172--174): ``The Gap Exponential Time Hypothesis
(Gap-ETH) strengthens ETH by postulating constants $\delta, \gamma > 0$
for which no $O(2^{\gamma n})$-time algorithm can distinguish a
satisfiable 3-CNF from one in which every assignment violates at least a
$\delta$ fraction of the clauses, even when $m = O(n)$ [Din16, MR17],''
citing \citet{Dinur2016} and \citet{ManurangsiRaghavendra2017}; and
(lines 175--176) ``The randomized version rules out an algorithm with
the same running time that distinguishes the two cases with probability
at least $2/3$ on every promised input [CCK$^+$20, Conjecture 2.5],''
citing \citet{Chalermsook2020}. In words: it should be hard even to tell
a satisfiable formula from one that is far from satisfiable. Its
denial: for every rate $\gamma$ there is an algorithm within
$O(2^{\gamma n})$ that tells the two apart, already when the number of
clauses is proportional to $n$. Exact decision tells them apart, since a
formula in which every assignment violates some clauses is
unsatisfiable; and the verified machine at $k = 3$ decides within
$O(2^{\gamma n})$ for every $\gamma$, at every number of clauses (item
2). The promise is never used: the machine answers correctly on every
input, promised or not, with probability $1$, which refutes the
randomised form by item 1.

\paragraph{NSETH.}
As stated (p.~4, lines 181--183): ``The Nondeterministic Strong
Exponential Time Hypothesis (NSETH) asserts that, for every
$\varepsilon > 0$, some fixed $k$ admits no nondeterministic
$2^{(1-\varepsilon)n}\allowbreak(n+m)^{O(1)}$-time algorithm for deciding whether a
$k$-CNF is unsatisfiable, or equivalently whether a $k$-DNF is a
tautology [CGI$^+$16],'' citing \citet{Carmosino2016}. (A $k$-DNF is the mirror image of a $k$-CNF, an ``or'' of small ``and''s, and a tautology is a formula true under every assignment.) In words: even an algorithm allowed to guess should not be able to certify
unsatisfiability with a saving over $2^n$. Its denial: there is a saving
that every fixed $k$ allows, for a guessing algorithm. The verified
machine's answer bit is \texttt{false} exactly when the count is zero
(lines 205--207), so it decides unsatisfiability deterministically within
the SETH bound of the previous paragraph for every $\varepsilon$ and
every $k$; and a deterministic machine is a guessing machine that never
guesses, so every convention for nondeterministic time is met. The
manuscript's ``decides both satisfiability and unsatisfiability'' (line
188) is this.

\paragraph{\#ETH.}
As stated (p.~4, lines 204--205): ``The counting Exponential Time
Hypothesis (\#ETH) asserted that exactly counting the satisfying
assignments of a 3-CNF requires $2^{\Omega(n)}$ deterministic time
[DHM$^+$14, Section 1],'' citing \citet{Dell2014}. Its denial: for every
rate $\delta > 0$ there is a deterministic algorithm within
$O(2^{\delta n})$ that outputs the exact count. The verified machine at
$k = 3$ halts with the count on its count tape, a binary number of at
most $n + 1$ bits (lines 203--204, 208--209), within the same bound. This
is the one hypothesis for which the yes-or-no bit is not enough and the
count tape is what refutes it. It is also the hypothesis the comparator
statement was written for: the count is the theorem's first conclusion
and the decision its corollary.

\paragraph{The bounded-error randomised ETH and SETH.}
The manuscript names them by citation only (p.~4, lines 190--191):
``[DHM$^+$14, Section 1]'' for ETH \citep{Dell2014} and ``[CDL$^+$16,
Definition 2.1]'' for SETH \citep{Cygan2016}. In each, the algorithm
may toss coins and must be right with probability clearly above one
half on every input. A deterministic algorithm is such an algorithm
that is right with probability $1$ (item 1), so the refutations of ETH
and SETH above refute these forms by the same machine, with no change
to the bound. We have not re-read the two sources' definitions; we
state the forms as the manuscript cites them, and a reader who needs the
exact error threshold or the exact model of the source should go to the
cited section and definition.

\paragraph{What the manuscript places beside the list.}
Two sentences follow the refutation paragraph (p.~4, lines 191--194):
the result ``does not conflict with unconditional exponential lower
bounds for restricted proof systems such as regular resolution [BI13],''
and ``In 2016, Ryan Williams refuted the Merlin--Arthur version of SETH
(MA-SETH) by giving a protocol that certifies $k$-CNF unsatisfiability
in $2^{n/2}\,\mathrm{poly}(n,m)$ time [Wil16, Theorem 3.3]''
\citep{Williams2016}. Both are context. The first says that a lower bound proved outright for one restricted way of writing proofs of unsatisfiability (``regular resolution'' is one such way) is not a statement about algorithms in general and is not touched. The second says that one member of the family, the version in which the proof of unsatisfiability may be checked with the help of randomness (a Merlin--Arthur protocol), was already false before this result. Neither is used below.

\subsection{What a derived lower bound loses}
\label{sec:consequences-lower-bounds}

The manuscript's \S1.1.3 opens with a list of lower bounds that were
proved from ETH or from SETH (p.~5, lines 213--225), for problems with
exponential algorithms and for problems with polynomial ones, and then
says what its result does to them (lines 226--227):
\begin{quote}\small
``Our refutation of ETH and SETH removes one basis for the hardness
claims above. Some of the same lower bounds follow from weaker
hypotheses that may still hold.''
\end{quote}
We say what that means in general terms, for a theorem of either of the
two standard shapes,
\begin{align*}
  \text{(E)}&\quad \text{ETH} \Rightarrow \text{no } 2^{o(n)} \text{ algorithm for } X,\\
  \text{(S)}&\quad \text{SETH} \Rightarrow \text{no } (c - \varepsilon)^{k} \text{ algorithm for } Y \text{ for any } \varepsilon > 0,
\end{align*}
where $n$ and $k$ are whatever measure of the input the theorem names
and $c$ is its constant. In words: (E) says that if ETH holds, problem
$X$ has no subexponential algorithm; (S) says that if SETH holds, no
algorithm for problem $Y$ beats the base $c$. Nothing below depends on
what $X$ and $Y$ are.

\paragraph{The theorem stands; its premise is false; it derives nothing.}
A theorem of shape (E) or (S) is an ``if \ldots{} then''. It is proved
by a reduction: a recipe that turns a fast algorithm for $X$ or $Y$ into
a fast algorithm for $k$-SAT. The recipe is unaffected by anything in
the verified statement; the ``if \ldots{} then'' remains a theorem. What
has changed is that its ``if'' is now known to be false
(Section~\ref{sec:consequences-hypotheses}), so the theorem can no
longer be applied: it derives nothing about $X$ or $Y$. That is the
whole of what ``removes one basis'' means.

\paragraph{The conclusion is not refuted.}
A false ``if'' does not make the ``then'' false. The statement ``no
$2^{o(n)}$ algorithm for $X$'' and the statement ``no
$(c-\varepsilon)^k$ algorithm for $Y$'' are untouched by anything in the
comparator statement, which speaks about $k$-CNF formulas and nothing
else. To refute either, one would need an algorithm for $X$ or $Y$, and
the verified statement supplies one only through a reduction in the
opposite direction to the one the lower bound used. The manuscript says
exactly this (p.~5, lines 237--238): ``To obtain a faster algorithm for
a target problem from our SAT algorithm, we need a reduction in the
other direction, from that problem to fixed-width SAT, that controls the
number of variables.'' And it places the general caveat on itself at
p.~2, lines 95--99:
\begin{quote}\small
``Note that runtime bounds of the form $2^{o(n)}$ do not transfer under
general polynomial-time reductions ($2^{n^{o(1)}}$ would be needed for
this), so our result does not imply that general NP problems admit
subexponential time algorithms.''
\end{quote}
So for shape (E): a subexponential algorithm for $X$ follows only from a
reduction of $X$ to fixed-width SAT in which the number of SAT variables
is proportional to $X$'s own size measure, and the manuscript gives one
such transfer (below). For shape (S): an algorithm for $Y$ with base below $c$ would need the SAT instance to be solved with an exponent a fixed fraction below $k \log_2 c$; the verified exponent is $A$ times $\rho$ of the SAT instance's variable count, $A$ is unknown with no value below $257$ on record, and nothing of the kind follows (Appendix~\ref{app:tech-hypotheses}). No
statement of shape ``no $(c-\varepsilon)^k$ algorithm'' is refuted by
anything in this paper or, so far as we can read, in the manuscript.

\paragraph{Hardness as a classification.}
A statement of the form ``$Y$ is SETH-hard'' (or ETH-hard) records that
a reduction exists from $k$-SAT to $Y$ with the stated control of the
size measure. The reduction still exists, and the statement is still
true as a statement about reductions. What it used to convey, that a
fast algorithm for $Y$ is unlikely because it would refute SETH, it no
longer conveys: a fast algorithm for $Y$ would refute SETH, and SETH is
refuted. The classification is now a fact about reductions and no
longer a fact about $Y$'s running time.

\paragraph{The weaker hypotheses the manuscript names are untouched.}
The manuscript gives two (p.~5, lines 229--236). The first is the
Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture, which it states and then relates to SETH:
``The reduction from SAT to Orthogonal Vectors shows that SETH implies
OVC [Wil05, VW19]'' (lines 231--232). That implication is a theorem
with a false premise, like (E) and (S): it says nothing about OVC either
way, and nothing in the verified statement is about orthogonal vectors.
The second is the form of SETH for formulas of unrestricted width, which
we quote with the manuscript's list of the problems it covers elided
(lines 233--236):
\begin{quote}\small
``The lower bounds for [\ldots] also follow from the hypothesis that
unrestricted CNF-SAT admits no $2^{(1-\delta)n}\,\mathrm{poly}(n,m)$-time
algorithm for any constant $\delta > 0$ [Bri14, RV13, LMS18]. Theorem
1.1 gives a running-time guarantee for each fixed clause width $k$,
while this hypothesis allows the width to grow with $n$.''
\end{quote}
The verified statement cannot reach this hypothesis, and the reason is
visible in the order of its words (Section~\ref{sec:statement}): the
machine and the constants $C, A, S, d, e$ are chosen after $k$, and
nothing in the statement bounds how they grow with $k$. For a formula
whose width grows with $n$ there is no single machine and no single
bound in the statement at all; even the trivial observation that every
$k$-CNF is a CNF runs the wrong way, since the hypothesis speaks about
all widths at once. So the unrestricted-width hypothesis, and every
lower bound that follows from it, is exactly where it was.

\paragraph{What a transferred algorithm inherits.}
The manuscript's one positive transfer is Set Splitting with sets of size at most $k$ (given small sets over a universe of elements, colour the elements with two colours so that no set is all one colour; p.~5, lines 239--243): one variable per element and
two clauses per set give a $k$-CNF with ``one variable per universe
element, so for fixed $k$ Theorem 1.1 gives subexponential time in the
universe size, up to a polynomial input-size factor,'' citing
\citet{Cygan2016}'s Observation~3.6. Section~\ref{sec:beyond} tags this
supported: the encoding is on paper, the counter is the verified one.
What such a transfer inherits is the verified guarantee on the SAT
instance it builds, $C(L+1)^d\,2^{A\rho(n')}$ with $n'$ the number of
SAT variables, and therefore the title's limitation with it: at $A = 1$
the exponential factor is above $2^{n'}$ for every
$50 \le n' < e^{5504}$ (Table~\ref{tab:runtime-scale}), with $C, A, d$
unknown. In general terms: an algorithm obtained from the verified
statement through a variable-controlling reduction is a subexponential
algorithm whose verified guarantee is weaker, at every practical size,
than trying every assignment of the SAT instance it constructs. That is
not a defect of the transfer; it is the same sentence as the title,
read through a reduction.

\subsection{What this does not claim}
\label{sec:consequences-not}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No statement ``no $(c-\varepsilon)^k$ algorithm'' is
  refuted}, for any problem, any size measure or any constant $c$. Such
  a statement, proved from SETH, has lost its proof and kept its truth
  value, which is unknown.
\item \textbf{No statement ``no $2^{o(n)}$ algorithm for $X$'' is
  refuted} either, unless $X$ has a reduction to fixed-width SAT that
  controls the number of variables; the manuscript names one such
  problem (Set Splitting) and we add none.
\item \textbf{The unrestricted-width hypothesis is untouched}, and so is
  the Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture, and so is every lower bound proved
  from either; the verified statement is for each fixed $k$ and says
  nothing about widths that grow with $n$.
\item \textbf{Nothing about any specific problem's complexity} beyond
  what the manuscript's \S1.1.1 and \S1.1.3 state and we quote. The
  problems the manuscript lists at lines 213--225 and 233--235 are not
  discussed here, and no example from any other setting is given, by
  the scope of this paper.
\item \textbf{No re-reading of the sources.} Each hypothesis is stated
  in the form the manuscript gives it, with the manuscript's citation;
  we did not check that the cited sections and definitions state it in
  the same words. If a source's form is stronger than the manuscript's,
  the refutation of the manuscript's form may not reach it, and the
  reader should check the source.
\item \textbf{About the limit only.} Every refutation here denies a
  statement about the limit and uses only that $\rhon$ grows more
  slowly than $n$. None says that any machine decides or counts
  anything fast at any size; the constants are as unknown here as in
  Section~\ref{sec:statement}, and the verified bound is above $2^n$ on
  every $50 \le n < e^{5504}$.
\item \textbf{Nothing beyond the verified statement.} The manuscript's
  sharper exponents (Section~\ref{sec:beyond}) are not used; where the
  manuscript derives a consequence from them, it is not a consequence
  of what is verified and is not claimed here.
\end{itemize}
