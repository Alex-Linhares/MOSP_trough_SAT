\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A factory makes a set of patterns, one at a time, and each customer's order
needs some of them. A customer's stack opens when the first pattern the
customer needs is made, and it closes when the last one is made. The
\emph{minimization of open stacks problem} (MOSP) asks for the order of the
patterns that keeps the fewest stacks open at any one time. In the cutting
industry, where the problem arose, the patterns are cutting patterns and the
customers are piece types \citep{Yanasse1997b}. Figure~\ref{fig:example}(a)
gives a small instance. Six customers, $a$ to $f$, need four patterns:
$P_1=\{a,b\}$, $P_2=\{b,c,d\}$, $P_3=\{c,d,e\}$ and $P_4=\{e,f\}$. Made in
order A, $P_1P_2P_3P_4$, the patterns keep at most 3 stacks open. Made in order
B, $P_1P_3P_4P_2$, they keep 5 open, because customer $b$ is served first but
waits for $P_2$, which comes last. Of the 24 orders, 2 reach 3 (order A and its
reverse), 16 give 4 and 6 give 5. MOSP is NP-hard
\citep{Linhares2001,LY2002}.

The same matrix is a problem in VLSI design. In a \emph{gate matrix layout}
the patterns are gates, drawn as vertical lines in some order, and the
customers are nets. Each net is a horizontal wire from the first gate it
connects to the last, and wires that overlap must lie on different horizontal
tracks. The task is to order the gates so that the fewest tracks suffice.
Figure~\ref{fig:example}(b) and (c) draw the two orders. The wires are the same;
only the gate order changes which wires overlap. Order A packs into 3 tracks
and order B needs 5. The number of tracks a gate order needs is its
\emph{density}, the most wires crossing any one gate, and that is exactly the
number of open stacks there. So the two problems compute the same number from
the same matrix \citep[Proposition~2]{LY2002}.

The same matrix is also a graph. Make each customer a vertex, and join two
customers when some pattern needs both, so that each pattern becomes a clique
(Figure~\ref{fig:example}(d)). Write down, for order A, the set of open stacks
at each position: $\{a,b\}$, $\{b,c,d\}$, $\{c,d,e\}$, $\{e,f\}$. These sets
cover every vertex and every edge, and each customer appears in a run of
consecutive sets. In graph theory such a sequence is a \emph{path
decomposition}, its sets are \emph{bags}, and its width is the size of its
largest bag minus one. The least width over all path decompositions is the
\emph{pathwidth} of the graph, here 2. The construction works in both
directions, so the fewest open stacks is always the pathwidth plus one
(Theorem~\ref{thm:mosp}).

\citet{LY2002} collected twelve such problems, from operations research, VLSI
design and graph theory, in the table that Table~\ref{tab:ly2002} reproduces.
Each computes, on corresponding inputs, ``a function $f(\Pi)$ that is either
equal to the number of open stacks or closely related to it (plus or minus
one)'' \citep[p.~1764]{LY2002}. The table gives a reference for each problem,
but no proofs and no graph of which problem reduces to which. ``Plus or minus
one'' has two readings. Under the \emph{literal} reading, $f$ is within one of
the number of open stacks on every input. Under the \emph{fixed-offset}
reading, $f$ equals the number of open stacks plus one constant, the same on
every input. Only the second makes solving one problem the same as solving
another. We call the twelve \emph{the pathwidth complex}, because pathwidth is
both the name the literature uses most (Section~\ref{sec:names}) and the
quantity through which every true relation in the table is proved
(Section~\ref{sec:equiv}). This paper asks: \emph{which of the equalities in
the Linhares--Yanasse table are true, and what follows when results,
algorithms and benchmarks are moved between the twelve problems through
pathwidth?}

The table has a history that explains why the question is still open. It was
first printed in the first author's doctoral thesis
\citep[Table~2.1, p.~13]{Linhares2001}, supervised by the second author, which proved MOSP
NP-hard and equal to gate matrix layout. Its rows were collected over the years
of the doctorate, while reading the literature of each field. Each came with a
reference and nothing else. No row's relation to open stacks was checked, no
published proof was restated, and no graph said which problem reduces to which.
The table appeared again, row for row, as Table~1 of \citet{LY2002}, and it has
been read since as a statement of equivalence.
\citet{GarciaDeLaBandaStuckey2007}, for instance, credit the speed of their
search in this journal to ``the fact that the problem is fixed parameter
tractable (Linhares and Yanasse 2002)''. So the table is already used to
justify algorithms, and whether it is true matters. If it is true, every result
about any one of the twelve problems transfers to all the others. Looking back,
the table was the thesis's most important finding, and this article does what
the thesis did not: it states each problem precisely, proves what is true and
refutes what is not. The communities that could have checked the table
rarely read each other: of 844 works that cite the table's papers,
only six cite both an open-stacks paper and a graph-theory paper
(Section~\ref{sec:names}).

\paragraph{Three more members.} The table is also incomplete. Progressive
pebbling, a game in which pebbles are placed on the vertices of a graph and
each vertex is pebbled once, needs exactly pathwidth plus one pebbles
\citep[Thm~3.1]{LY2002ref10}, and Lengauer's black-white pebbling of a
directed acyclic graph equals the pathwidth of its undirected version plus one
\citep[Thm~2]{LY2002ref14}. Both theorems are in papers that the table
already cites, for its edge-search and edge-separation rows, yet pebbling
itself is not among its problems. \citet[(5.1)]{BRST1991} search for a fugitive
who hides in the vertices, not the edges, and need exactly pathwidth plus one
searchers on every graph, including the edgeless graphs where the table's node
search row fails. From numerical linear algebra, the frontal method for a
sparse symmetric matrix stores, at each step, the \emph{wavefront}: the rows
still active, the current row together with every later row that has a nonzero
in a column already eliminated \citep[\S2.1]{KumfertPothen1997}. Ordering the
matrix so that the largest wavefront is small is the problem of the table: the
least maximum wavefront is the pathwidth of the matrix's graph plus one, on
every nonempty matrix. We add the three as the last rows of
Table~\ref{tab:ly2002}, marked as new, and prove the pebbling and wavefront
relations in Lean (Appendix~\ref{app:thirteenth}). For pebbling and the vertex
search, what is new is the membership: the equivalences were published, but
the table did not include them. For the wavefront the equality itself is ours.
It is elementary, the wavefront being narrowness read in another form, but no
source we hold states it.

\paragraph{What we find.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The table, settled.} Eight of the twelve rows are exact, the
  pathwidth row itself counted as one of them: the other seven equal pathwidth
  or pathwidth plus one on every input, outside degenerate cases such as an
  empty graph. Two rows, split bandwidth and edge
  search, lie in bands one and two wide. Two rows, PLA folding and edge
  separation, are false as stated, with counterexamples. A variant of each
  false row is exact. Three problems outside the table are exact members
  too: progressive pebbling, a vertex search, and the maximum wavefront of
  sparse matrix elimination (Section~\ref{sec:equiv}).
\item \emph{One name.} The literature has settled on ``pathwidth'', which has
  more than three times as many relevant works as the other eleven names
  together. Besides pathwidth, the most active community over 2005--2024 is the
  open-stacks one, and the exact search of Section~\ref{sec:search} came
  from it
  (Section~\ref{sec:names}).
\item \emph{A repaired search.} The best exact algorithm for MOSP, the search of
  \citet{ChuStuckey2009}, is also a pathwidth algorithm. Two of its published
  dominance theorems are false, as are their later restatements
  \citep{Chu2011,Fink2012}. The correct rule was already known in the exact
  pathwidth literature, and we show that it can be tested by one bipartite
  matching per candidate. Our repaired search is proved sound. Over each
  benchmark set it changes the total number of search nodes by at most 2\%. On
  every benchmark the published rules happen to answer correctly, but we
  exhibit a 34-customer instance on which they report a wrong optimum
  (Section~\ref{sec:search}).
\item \emph{Benchmarks re-certified, and a dataset.} Our repaired search
  re-proved all 115 benchmark optima that rested on the published rules, and
  none changed (as of 9 October 2026). We release 17,714 graphs, distinct up to
  isomorphism, from twenty benchmark collections of the twelve problems; 16,087
  have a certified pathwidth with a layout that attains it, and so a certified
  value for every exact problem in the table (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{How to read the paper.} Section~\ref{sec:equiv} states the twelve
problems and proves what is true. Section~\ref{sec:names} measures which names
and communities the literature uses. Section~\ref{sec:search} reads the exact
open-stacks search as a pathwidth algorithm, finds the two false rules and
proves our repaired search sound. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports its cost
and the dataset, and Section~\ref{sec:closing} answers the question above.
Every theorem we state is machine-checked in the Lean~4 proof assistant. The
reader need not read Lean: each proof is given or cited in the text, and
Appendix~\ref{app:lean} maps each result to its formal statement.
Appendix~\ref{app:machinery-all} holds the supporting material,
Appendix~\ref{app:names} the bibliometric method, and the data,
code and proofs are described at the end of the paper (page~\pageref{sec:data}).
