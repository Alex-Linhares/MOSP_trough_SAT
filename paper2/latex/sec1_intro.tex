\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In the minimization of open stacks problem (MOSP), patterns are cut in some
order, every piece type has a stack that opens with the first pattern that
contains it and closes with the last, and the task is to choose the order that
keeps the fewest stacks open at once. The first author's doctoral thesis
\citep{Linhares2001}, supervised by the second, was about this problem and its
relatives in industrial pattern sequencing. Its complexity chapter proves two
things. First, MOSP is NP-hard, by a reduction from modified cutwidth: padding
the edge--vertex incidence matrix of a graph so that every column has the same
sum $C$ gives an instance whose number of open stacks is the modified cutwidth
plus $C$ \citep[Proposition~2.1 and Lemma~2.2]{Linhares2001}. Second, the number
of open stacks equals the number of tracks of a gate matrix layout, ``trivially
from their definitions'' (Proposition~2.2). From the second it draws, as a
corollary of the fixed-parameter tractability of gate matrix layout
\citep{FellowsLangston1987}, that MOSP is fixed-parameter tractable
(Corollary~2.1).

Between those two results the thesis prints a table (Table~2.1, p.~13). Its
twelve rows are problems from operations research, VLSI design and graph theory
that compute, on corresponding inputs, ``a function $f(\Phi)$ that is either
equal to the number of open stacks or closely related to it (plus or minus
one)''. The rows were collected over the years of the doctorate, while reading
the literature of each field. Each comes with a reference and nothing else. No
row's relation to open stacks was checked, no published proof was restated,
and no graph says which problem reduces to which. The table appeared again,
row for row and with the same sentence introducing it, as Table~1 of
\citet{LY2002}, and it has been read since as a statement of equivalence.
\citet{GarciaDeLaBandaStuckey2007}, for instance, credit the speed of their
fixed-$N$ search in this journal to ``the fact that the problem is fixed
parameter tractable (Linhares and Yanasse 2002)''. The thesis's own summary of
findings (\S7.1) does not mention the table at all.

Looking back, the table was the thesis's most important finding. Twelve
problems from three disciplines, each studied independently, are claimed to
be one problem, and if that is true, then every result about any one of them
transfers to all the others. This article does what the thesis did not: it
states each problem precisely, proves what is true and refutes what is not,
and then takes the strongest exact algorithm in the family through the same
check.

\paragraph{The pathwidth complex.} \citet{LY2002} collected, in their
Table~1, twelve problems from operations research, VLSI design and graph theory
that had been studied independently and that compute, on corresponding inputs,
``a function $f(\Pi)$ that is either equal to the number of open stacks or
closely related to it (plus or minus one)'' \citep[p.~1764]{LY2002}. The table
gave references, but no proofs and no graph. Table~\ref{tab:ly2002} reproduces
it as printed. We call this cluster \emph{the pathwidth complex}. Pathwidth is
both the name the literature uses most (Section~\ref{sec:names}) and the
quantity through which every relation in the table is proved
(Section~\ref{sec:equiv}). Of the twelve rows, one is pathwidth itself, seven
are exactly pathwidth plus a constant, two lie within a band of width one or
two, and two are false as Table~1 states them (Table~\ref{tab:master}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A census of how much each of the twelve names is used, after removing the
  search hits that use the words in other senses; pathwidth has more relevant
  works than the other eleven names together, three times over
  (Section~\ref{sec:names}).
\item Each problem of Table~1 stated as an instance and a question, with the
  graph it lives on and its exact relation to pathwidth, every relation proved
  in Lean; two of Table~1's rows are false as stated (Section~\ref{sec:equiv}).
\item The exact open-stacks search of \citet{ChuStuckey2009} read as a
  vertex-separation search: two of its published dominance theorems are false,
  as are their restatements by \citet{Chu2011} and \citet{Fink2012}; the repair,
  its cost, and a Lean proof that the repaired search is sound; and a proof
  object for its refutations with an independent checker
  (Section~\ref{sec:search}).
\item A dataset of 17,714 isomorphism classes from twenty collections, 16,087 of
  them with a certified pathwidth and a witness layout, which through
  Section~\ref{sec:equiv} answers every problem in Table~1's exact core
  (Section~\ref{sec:dataset}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Notation.} Graphs are finite and simple, $G=(V,E)$, $n=|V|$. A
\emph{layout} is a bijection $L:V\to\{1,\dots,n\}$. For a 0/1 matrix $M$ with
rows $R$ (customers, nets, piece types) and columns $C$ (patterns, gates,
products), the \emph{MOSP graph} $G_M$ has vertex set $R$, with $r\sim r'$ when
some column has a 1 in both rows; each column becomes a clique on its rows. For
an order $\pi$ of the columns, row $r$ is \emph{active} at position $j$ if it
has 1s in columns $x,y$ with $\pi(x)\le j\le\pi(y)$.
